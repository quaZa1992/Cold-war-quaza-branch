# IMPERIA COLD WAR — Cuba Bilateral Historical Precedents, 1970–1990

This memo identifies four historically grounded deal routes that can use existing bilateral-decision machinery without adding focuses. The historical record is separated from the proposed alternate-history contract and its balance logic. A requirement that both states be at peace and not mutually sanctioned is a gameplay eligibility rule, not a claim about how the historical agreements were negotiated. Likewise, counterpart consent should be an explicit decision: the precedent shows plausible bargaining, not automatic agreement.

## Mexico: regional oil exchange and industrial cooperation

**Historical record.** Cuba and Mexico built a formal framework for economic and industrial cooperation in an agreement signed in Havana on 21 August 1975. A broader General Agreement on Cooperation was signed at Tlatelolco on 27 January 1978. It coordinated existing and future agreements in economic sectors, education, science and technology, industry, trade, fishing, tourism, services, and finance through a joint intergovernmental General Commission. The framework specified cooperation of reciprocal interest, with new activities coordinated through the commission rather than granted unconditionally. [Mexico–Cuba General Agreement, United Nations Treaty Series, signed 27 January 1978](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%201379/v1379.pdf)

In a 1978 meeting with President Carter, Mexican President José López Portillo described an existing triangular arrangement: Mexico shipped oil to Cuba while the Soviet Union shipped oil to certain Mexican markets in Europe and Asia. López Portillo said it saved transport costs and connected Cuba more closely to the Latin American economy. This is a primary record of a leader’s account in a U.S. presidential conversation, not a published contract or evidence of a fixed volume or guaranteed long-term supply. [FRUS, Carter–López Portillo conversation, 14 February 1978](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d131)

**Alternate-history deal route.** Offer a renewable oil-shipping and industrial-cooperation protocol: Mexican crude or refined-product deliveries to Cuba are matched by Soviet deliveries into designated Mexican markets, alongside a small list of mutually approved industrial projects administered through the 1978-style commission. Mexico must accept the arrangement. The Cuban counterpart spirit could represent improved fuel access, freight efficiency, and regional trade links; the Mexican spirit could represent negotiated transit/export revenue and regional influence. The costs should include Cuban reliance on both a Mexican supply channel and Soviet performance, plus Mexican exposure to a politically sensitive triangular deal. A refusal should leave existing relations untouched. Avoid free oil, automatic Soviet compliance, or a claim that the 1978 framework guaranteed this particular arrangement.

## Spain: commodity-for-capital-goods trade and development credit

**Historical record.** Spain and Cuba signed a commercial agreement and protocols on 23 January 1979, applying the commercial treaty provisionally from 1 February 1979. The 1979 protocol set reciprocal trade expectations: Cuba was to supply Spain at least 3,500 metric tonnes of nickel sinter and oxide, while Spanish cash exports under the payments mechanism were to reach at least the equivalent of US$35 million, with US$50 million as the stated target. Spain also offered credit facilities for Cuban capital-goods purchases and a government credit through its development-aid fund. These were commercial and credit terms, not a grant of machinery without payment. [Spain–Cuba commercial agreement and 1979 protocol, BOE-A-1979-7815](https://boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-1979-7815)

**Alternate-history deal route.** Offer a multi-year nickel-and-fish supply schedule against Spanish capital equipment, supported by negotiated supplier credit and a payments-clearing protocol. Spain must approve the credit and commodity schedule. Cuba could gain construction/industrial capacity and access to a Western market; Spain could gain secured commodity supply, equipment sales, and an institutional commercial channel. Balance the Cuban benefit against debt service, delivery obligations, and the opportunity cost of committing export goods to a fixed buyer. The Spanish benefit should carry credit exposure and a small diplomatic/economic return, not an implausible military alignment. A counterpart refusal should close the offer without creating obligations.

## Canada: locomotives and the limits of commercial autonomy

**Historical record.** On 13 February 1974, Canada asked the United States for an exemption to permit a US-owned Canadian subsidiary to export locomotives to Cuba. The proposed contract was worth US$13 million and was important to Quebec employment. Because U.S. regulations restricted U.S. firms’ foreign subsidiaries trading with Cuba without a Treasury license, and prior exemptions had been small, the contract required a U.S. decision. President Nixon rejected approval. The episode demonstrates a concrete Canadian interest in trade alongside real extraterritorial constraints; it does not support depicting Canadian trade as unrestricted or the locomotive sale as completed. [FRUS memorandum to President Nixon, 14 March 1974](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d279)

A 1978 U.S. government assessment also reported that Canada sold Cuba US$279 million in 1976, and noted Canadian Prime Minister Trudeau’s decision not to renew a US$10 million credit line and US$4.5 million in technical assistance amid disagreement over Cuba’s African policy. These figures and the reported decision show that commercial ties could coexist with policy limits and could be adjusted during diplomatic disputes. [FRUS, proposed Western trade and credit measures, 26 May 1978](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d32)

**Alternate-history deal route.** Offer a Canadian rail-equipment and maintenance contract, funded through a bilateral credit line and subject to a plausible export-control settlement where U.S.-owned firms or controlled components are involved. Canada’s explicit acceptance should be required; if the scenario models a U.S. veto/clearance, that should be a separate diplomatic condition rather than an automatic Canadian refusal. Cuba could receive transport capacity and maintenance expertise; Canada could gain industrial orders and a continuing service market. The reciprocal cost is credit exposure and political friction with Washington, especially if Cuban foreign policy has recently raised tensions. Do not present this deal as a permanent friendship pact or assume the 1974 license was granted.

## Soviet Union: commodity terms, energy security, and dependence

**Historical record.** A five-year Cuba–USSR trade agreement for 1976–1980 was signed on 6 February 1976. A 1986 World Bank working paper reconstructs the sugar agreements and finds the Soviet price for Cuban sugar was partly determined by Soviet oil supply to Cuba; it also documents the interaction between fixed bilateral terms and world-market prices. The paper is retrospective economic analysis, not the text of the bilateral agreement. [C. Suan Tan, World Bank, *Cuba-USSR Sugar Trade* (June 1986)](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/484211493317774279/pdf/multi-page.pdf)

The relationship could cushion price shocks but intensified concentration risk. A 1977 Congressional Record account says the Soviet sugar arrangement became unfavorable to Cuba when world sugar prices rose above its contracted level in the early 1970s, then more favorable after the 1975–76 price fall; Cuba sought hard currency by diversifying trade toward Japan and Spain. A 1979 U.S. diplomatic assessment estimated that roughly three-quarters of Cuban exports went to the USSR, which supplied around 60 percent of imports, and described the subsidy as economically substantial. Those are U.S. government estimates, not neutral Cuban national accounts. [Congressional Record, 16 May 1977](https://www.congress.gov/95/crecb/1977/05/16/GPO-CRECB-1977-pt12-6.pdf); [FRUS, Cuba and the Soviet Union, 9 September 1979](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v06/d220)

**Alternate-history deal route.** Keep this separate from the existing technical-assistance, CMEA, or air-defense route by making it a commodity-pricing and supply protocol: guaranteed minimum sugar/nickel offtake and oil deliveries in return for a longer planning horizon, delivery priorities, and coordinated industrial-import schedules. Both governments should consent. The Cuban return could be greater fuel and import predictability; the Soviet return could be secure commodities, planning influence, and lower freight/market uncertainty. The Cuban downside should reflect narrower access to hard-currency markets, concentration on a single buyer and supplier, and possible losses if world prices move against fixed terms. Do not make the deal a permanent, cost-free subsidy or imply that the 1976 agreement removed those risks.

## Implementation guidance

These are four candidate bilateral offers, not four historical inevitabilities. A compact implementation can give each offer one initiating decision, one recipient acceptance/refusal decision, and persistent reciprocal country spirits only after acceptance. Use effects to reflect negotiated material exchanges and the counterpart’s own gain. Peace/no-mutual-sanctions, contract duration, and whether a particular government is willing to negotiate are scenario rules. Historical evidence supports plausible interest and institutional forms; it does not establish that all four states would sign every proposed contract in every alternate timeline.
