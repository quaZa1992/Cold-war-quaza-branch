# IMPERIA COLD WAR: Cuban Foreign Policy Offers, 1970 to 1990

## Scope and interaction model

This is historical research for reciprocal diplomacy offers started by separate Cuban foreign-policy focuses. A Cuban focus may send a proposal, but only the other country's RP player should receive the acceptance or refusal decision. If accepted, each government should receive its own continuing benefit and cost. No agreement should be inferred from a date, a historical precedent, or a relationship score alone.

The facts below establish plausible interests, institutions, and terms. Proposed in-game versions are alternate history unless explicitly described as an actual historical agreement. Peace and absence of mutual sanctions are useful gameplay eligibility rules, not general historical preconditions. Several relationships involved wars or disputes, so those restrictions should apply to the new contract system rather than be presented as what happened in history.

## Twelve usable precedents

### 1. USSR and Cuba: sugar for oil, 1976 to 1980

**Fact.** A five-year trade agreement was signed on 6 February 1976. A World Bank study of the 1961 to 1983 trade record finds Soviet sugar prices partly linked to world prices, Cuba's trade balance within the socialist bloc, and Soviet oil supplies. A U.S. Congressional Record account says the fixed arrangement could disadvantage Cuba when free-market sugar prices rose above the Soviet price, and become more favorable when world prices fell. [World Bank, *Cuba-USSR Sugar Trade*, June 1986](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/484211493317774279/pdf/multi-page.pdf); [Congressional Record, 16 May 1977](https://www.congress.gov/95/crecb/1977/05/16/GPO-CRECB-1977-pt12-6.pdf)

**Possible offer.** Cuba proposes a longer sugar and nickel offtake schedule against Soviet petroleum deliveries and a pricing formula. Cuba gains more predictable fuel and exports; the USSR secures commodities and a planned supply relationship. The Cuban cost is dependence on one buyer and supplier, plus losses when contract terms lag world prices. It should be a renegotiable commodity contract, not a free Soviet subsidy.

### 2. USSR and Cuba: industrial construction and technical assistance, 1976 onward

**Fact.** A 1976 economic and technical assistance agreement expanded Soviet support. A 1979 UNIDO paper reports plans for an integrated steel mill, work on Cuba's first nuclear power plant, a Moa nickel project, and modernization of Punta Gorda. It reports Soviet-assisted enterprises in 1976 accounted for about 30 percent of finished rolled steel and 75 percent of nitrogen fertilizer. These are reported projects and output shares, not proof that all planned facilities were completed on schedule. [UNIDO, *Instruments and Methods for the Economic Co-operation and Integration of CMEA Member Countries*, 1979](https://downloads.unido.org/ot/46/92/4692383/00001-10000_09396.pdf)

**Possible offer.** Cuba seeks one defined industrial package, such as metallurgical modernization or fertilizer capacity, with an agreed Cuban raw-material supply and maintenance plan. Cuba gains production capacity; the Soviet side gains equipment contracts, commodity access, and influence through technical standards and planning. Costs include long build times, dependence on imported parts, hard-to-reverse planning commitments, and risk that Soviet technology is a poor fit. Keep this separate from generic technical-aid or air-defense offers.

### 3. Mexico and Cuba: intergovernmental economic and industrial projects, 1975 to 1978

**Fact.** The economic and industrial cooperation agreement was signed in Havana on 21 August 1975 and entered into force on 10 August 1976. It called for specific projects of mutual interest and an intergovernmental working group to define programs, implementation terms, and conditions. A broader general cooperation agreement signed on 27 January 1978 coordinated existing and future arrangements across industry, trade, fisheries, tourism, science, education, services, and finance through a joint commission. [Mexico's Foreign Affairs Secretariat treaty catalogue, 1975 agreement](https://cja.sre.gob.mx/tratadosmexico/tratados/eyJpdiI6ImR5V0VacG56QVwvQUVnM2xxUzFJTkNBPT0iLCJ2YWx1ZSI6Inh0R0tCYXpPTWNZN2tCU25oc2JDSFE9PSIsIm1hYyI6IjNiMzQ2NzZlOWYwMzVlMGU5N2ZkZWQ0M2IzZWZhMWJmM2QyY2JmYTNjMDI2YWY4OWJmMjE5ZWYzYjI5NmE4MjYifQ==); [Mexico-Cuba General Agreement, UN Treaty Series, signed 27 January 1978](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%201379/v1379.pdf)

**Possible offer.** Cuba proposes a commission-approved agricultural processing, fisheries, or port logistics project with specified reciprocal purchases. Cuba gains machinery, market access, or better food and input supply; Mexico gains trade and regional ties while securing agreed deliveries. Each project needs separate quantities, price, funding, and a local implementation schedule. The institutional precedent supports a project list and review commission, not an open-ended aid bonus.

### 4. Mexico, Cuba, and USSR: triangular oil exchange, described in 1978

**Fact.** During a 1978 meeting with President Carter, Mexico's President José López Portillo said Mexico had helped Cuba through an exchange in which Mexico shipped oil to Cuba and the USSR shipped oil to some Mexican markets in Europe and Asia. He said the arrangement reduced transport costs and connected Cuba more closely to Latin America. This is a firsthand account of a leader describing the arrangement in a U.S. presidential conversation, rather than a published contract with guaranteed volumes. [FRUS, Carter-López Portillo conversation, 14 February 1978](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d131)

**Possible offer.** Cuba asks Mexico to renew or expand a route-swapping arrangement. Cuba gains a shorter or more reliable supply channel and stronger regional links; Mexico gains better routing and political reach; the USSR gains access to selected Mexican markets. All affected states must assent. The downside is coordinated delivery risk: failure by any party disrupts the whole exchange. Do not present this as Mexican charity or automatic Soviet cooperation.

### 5. Spain and Cuba: nickel against capital goods and credit, 1979

**Fact.** Spain and Cuba signed a commercial agreement and protocols on 23 January 1979. The 1979 protocol specified a minimum Cuban supply to Spain of 3,500 metric tonnes of nickel sinter and oxide, at least US$35 million equivalent in Spanish cash exports under the payments mechanism, and a US$50 million target. Spain offered credit facilities and a government credit from its development-aid fund for Cuban capital-goods purchases. [Spain's Official Gazette, BOE-A-1979-7815](https://boe.es/diario_boe/txt.php?id=BOE-A-1979-7815)

**Possible offer.** Cuba offers nickel deliveries and agreed access to Spanish suppliers in return for equipment on credit. Cuba receives industrial goods and financing; Spain secures nickel, export orders, and a payments channel. Cuba pays through scheduled deliveries and debt service, with exposure if nickel output or export prices fall. Spain carries repayment risk. This is a strong reciprocal commercial offer, not military alignment or free development aid.

### 6. Canada and Cuba: rail equipment proposal, 1974

**Fact.** In February 1974 Canada requested U.S. approval for a US$13 million Canadian locomotive export to Cuba, citing employment in Quebec. The transaction involved a U.S.-owned Canadian subsidiary and required a U.S. Treasury license under the regulations then in force. President Nixon rejected approval. This records real Canadian commercial interest and the external legal constraint; it was not a completed sale. A separate 1978 U.S. report noted that Canada was a major Cuban trading partner in 1976, but also described Ottawa's decision not to renew a credit line and technical assistance amid disagreement over Cuba's actions in Africa. [FRUS memorandum on Canadian locomotive export, 14 March 1974](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve11p1/d279); [FRUS, proposed Western trade and credit measures, 26 May 1978](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d32)

**Possible offer.** Cuba proposes a locomotive, spare-parts, or rail-maintenance contract. Canada decides whether to participate; if a U.S.-owned firm or controlled components are involved, a separate U.S. clearance may be needed. Cuba gains transport capacity and service expertise; Canada gains orders and employment. Costs include credit exposure, U.S. political friction, and maintenance dependence. Label it a plausible alternate-history contract, not a historical agreement already made.

### 7. Cuba and the GDR: trade, machinery, science, and worker training, 1974 to 1978

**Fact.** During Erich Honecker's February 1974 Cuba visit, the two governments signed a declaration to strengthen friendship and cooperation. The GDR imported Cuban sugar and citrus and supplied technology and technical knowledge for Cuban industry. A declassified U.S. intelligence chronology records that a Cuba-GDR labor and wage agreement was signed in Havana on 20 October 1978 and provided training for Cubans at GDR industrial facilities. A 1979 UNIDO paper also records the GDR and other CMEA partners' participation in industrial development projects. [Contemporary SED declaration text, 1974](https://www.gvoon.de/dokumente-sed-ddr-1974-1975/seite-30-599518.html); [German History in Documents and Images, GDR visit and trade context](https://germanhistorydocs.org/en/two-germanies-1961-1989/east-german-government-delegation-visits-cuba-1974); [CIA Reading Room, Cuba chronology, 1978 entry](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP83B00225R000100050001-5.pdf); [UNIDO, 1979](https://downloads.unido.org/ot/46/92/4692383/00001-10000_09396.pdf)

**Possible offer.** Cuba proposes a training-and-equipment exchange tied to a specific citrus, printing, machinery, or industrial project, with Cuban goods supplied under a schedule. Cuba gains skills, equipment, and market access; the GDR gains tropical produce, export orders, and a reliable socialist partner. Costs include delivery and quality obligations, long logistics, and worker-placement conditions. Do not make all education, technical aid, or equipment free.

### 8. Cuba and the GDR: Thälmann island gesture, June and August 1972

**Fact.** During Fidel Castro's June 1972 East Berlin visit, he presented the island then called Cayo Blanco del Sur as a gift to the GDR and renamed it Isla Ernesto Thälmann; the shore was called Playa RDA. On 18 August 1972 a bust ceremony was held there. The episode was a symbolic dedication and renaming, not a legally effective transfer of Cuban sovereignty. In 2001, the German Foreign Office and Cuban Embassy were reported as rejecting a legal territorial claim and describing the episode as symbolic. [MDR, historical account citing the 1972 visit and official 2001 clarifications](https://www.mdr.de/geschichte/ddr/alltag/reisen-freizeit/kuba-insel-fidel-castro-100.html); [ARD Archive, broadcast of the Castro-Honecker meeting, 19 June 1972](https://www.swr.de/swrkultur/wissen/archivradio/fidel-castro-besucht-ddr-und-schenkt-ihr-eine-insel-100.html)

**Possible offer.** Treat the dedication as a ceremonial cultural or scientific access arrangement, with both governments acknowledging Cuba's continuing sovereignty. Cuba receives symbolic solidarity and perhaps reciprocal cultural exchange; the GDR receives a public friendship symbol and access for approved exchanges. There is no basis for a territorial handover, a permanent GDR garrison, or automatic island development revenue.

### 9. Cuba and the United States: fisheries and maritime boundary agreements, 1977

**Fact.** In 1977 the first direct official talks since 1961 produced written fisheries and preliminary maritime-boundary agreements. Cuba initiated talks in response to the U.S. 200-mile fishing zone, and the sides negotiated reciprocal arrangements. Cuba sought an equal standing in talks and wider normalization; the United States sought orderly fishing and maritime rules. This was a concrete area of mutual interest even while the trade embargo and larger political conflicts remained. [FRUS, fisheries initiative, 28 January 1977](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d1); [FRUS, U.S. policy paper summarizing the agreements, 1977](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d18)

**Possible offer.** Cuba proposes a fisheries and maritime-management agreement. Both sides gain predictable jurisdiction, conservation, and reduced encounter risk. Each accepts reporting and enforcement obligations; Cuba does not receive market normalization automatically, and the U.S. does not receive Cuban concessions on unrelated questions automatically.

### 10. Cuba and the United States: Interests Sections and gradual normalization, 1977

**Fact.** Carter's March 1977 directive authorized exploratory talks on a measured and reciprocal path toward reestablishing relations. It explicitly listed matters including fisheries, hijacking, human rights, Cuba's foreign activities, property compensation, possible trade, and a U.S. Interests Section in Havana. Both governments agreed to operating arrangements in May, and the Interests Sections opened on 1 September 1977 in the Swiss Embassy in Havana and the Czechoslovak Embassy in Washington. This restored official contact channels but did not restore full diplomatic relations or repeal the embargo. [Presidential Directive NSC-61, 15 March 1977](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d9); [FRUS, 1977 policy paper on next steps and reciprocal interests](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v23/d18)

**Possible offer.** Cuba proposes reciprocal Interests Section access, secure diplomatic premises, staff protections, and regular consultations. Cuba gains a channel to Washington and potential access to technology and markets; the United States gains direct contact, consular functions, and a forum for disputes. The deal is politically fragile and should bring only limited diplomatic benefits until separately negotiated questions are addressed. Do not describe it as complete normalization.

### 11. Cuba and the United States: migration normalization after Mariel, 1980 and 1984

**Fact.** The 1980 Mariel exodus was a crisis, not a bilateral migration treaty. U.S. State Department chronology records the April 1980 exodus and later U.S.-Cuban meetings on repatriation beginning in December 1980. The actual migration agreement was reached on 14 December 1984: Cuba agreed to accept the return of up to 2,746 Mariel entrants classified as excludable, while the United States agreed to issue up to 20,000 preference visas annually, outside immediate-relative visas. Implementation was suspended in 1985 after Radio Marti began broadcasting and revived in 1987. [U.S. Department of State, chronology of Cuban migration, 1958 to 1998](https://1997-2001.state.gov/regions/wha/cuba/migration_chron.html); [U.S. government report describing the 1984 pact and suspension/reinstatement](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/eoir/legacy/2013/06/13/CS_Cuba.pdf)

**Possible offer.** A post-crisis Cuban proposal can seek orderly family migration procedures, consular access, and return rules. Cuba gains an orderly channel and some return of nationals considered inadmissible; the United States gains a negotiated return process and reduced incentives for disorderly departures. Both incur political costs. Any exact visa quota, return categories, and enforcement mechanism should require the U.S. player's decision. Never attach a free population debit to announcing a crisis or portray 1984 terms as existing in 1980.

### 12. Angola and Ethiopia: separate civil cooperation and security offers

**Angola fact.** Cuba and Angola signed an Economic, Scientific, and Technical Cooperation Framework Agreement in Havana on 29 July 1976. It institutionalized Cuban assistance in health, education, industry, agriculture, and infrastructure; sector agreements covered Cuban medical and paramedical staff in Angolan hospitals and clinics and training in education and other fields. Scholarly archival research reports that the framework also established financial compensation for Cuban civil aid and economic benefits to Cuba, including sugar import quotas and fisheries cooperation. [Christine Hatzky, *Cubans in Angola: South-South Cooperation and Transfer of Knowledge, 1976-1991*, University of Wisconsin Press, 2015](https://www.in-cubadora.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/cubans-in-angola-south-south-cooperation-and-transfer-of-knowledge-1976e280931991.pdf); [Cuban bilateral-agreement calendar excerpt citing the 1976 ministry agreements](https://cendoc-paz.cihac-calas.fcs.ucr.ac.cr/fr/documents/521/document_pages/4386?search%5Bdocument_id%5D%5B%5D=521&search%5Bobject_type%5D=page&search%5Bsort_by%5D=date_asc)

**Angola possible offer.** Offer civilian teachers, clinicians, agricultural specialists, or engineers under a paid and mutually scheduled cooperation protocol. Angola gains urgently needed skilled staff and institutional capacity; Cuba gains compensation, trade access, and an allied regional partner. Costs include staff shortages at home, travel and security exposure, and Angolan reliance on foreign personnel. A separate military-adviser or volunteer offer must require Angolan acceptance and explicit exposure to casualties, escalation, and diplomatic backlash. Do not bundle combat deployment with medical aid or make the latter contingent on the former.

**Ethiopia fact.** Cuba and Ethiopia established diplomatic relations on 22 July 1975. In late 1977, Ethiopian ruler Mengistu sought increased support during his visit to Cuba, the GDR, and the USSR. A contemporaneous U.S. intelligence cable dated 14 December 1977 reported more than 400 additional Cuban military advisers/instructors arriving alongside Soviet support, a total then above 1,000 Cuban military personnel and 150 to 200 Cuban medical personnel. It said the Ethiopian military council had agreed to an increased Cuban role and that combat use was being planned. The cable is a declassified U.S. intelligence account; numbers are estimates, and it describes a transition from advising toward combat, not a simple civilian mission. [U.S. Embassy Addis Ababa cable establishing relations, 22 July 1975](https://wikileaks.org/plusd/cables/1975ADDIS08717_b.html); [FRUS, CIA information cable, 14 December 1977](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v06/d66)

**Ethiopia possible offer.** A medical, training, or military-adviser offer can be proposed only when Ethiopia has requested it and must be accepted by the Ethiopian RP player. Ethiopia could gain health services, training, and military expertise; Cuba could gain diplomatic influence and a strategic partner. Costs include regional isolation, entanglement in the Ogaden and Eritrean conflicts, escalation into combat, and the difficulty of withdrawing personnel once deployed. Ethiopia should gain a decision on scope and duration; combat must never be implied by accepting only medical aid.

## Selection recommendation

The most distinct, reciprocal trade and institution routes are Mexico's commission-based projects, the Mexico-USSR oil swap, Spain's nickel-credit protocol, Canada rail equipment, and the USSR commodity or industrial package. The GDR offers both a trade-training route and the symbolic island dedication, provided the island event explicitly preserves Cuban sovereignty. The U.S. options are narrow and staged: fisheries, Interests Sections, and later migration procedures. Angola supports separate paid civilian cooperation and accepted security deployment; Ethiopia supports a high-cost, recipient-requested adviser path with an explicit risk of escalation. These are proposals for bilateral focuses and recipient-only decisions, not a list of treaties every 1970 start government is assumed to accept.
