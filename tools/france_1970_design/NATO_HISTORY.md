# France and NATO: historical constraints for a 1970 Gaullist sub-tree

## Starting position in January 1970

France remained a founding party to the 1949 North Atlantic Treaty. It retained the treaty's collective-defence commitment, including Article 5, and continued to participate in NATO's political bodies. In March 1966, de Gaulle announced withdrawal from NATO's integrated military command structure, demanded removal of foreign forces and headquarters from French territory, and rejected allied control of French forces and the independent nuclear deterrent. France was therefore outside the integrated command and key military-planning bodies, but it had not left NATO or cancelled Article 5. “France left NATO in 1966” is historically false shorthand. NATO's historical account describes this distinction directly: [France and NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/nato-history/history-by-theme/my-country-and-nato/france-and-nato).

The distinction matters for the Nuclear Planning Group. Established in December 1966, the NPG handled detailed nuclear planning and was originally limited to countries participating in NATO's integrated military structure. France did not participate. The 1966 communiqué also records a Nuclear Defence Affairs Committee open to all NATO members, distinct from the restricted NPG: [NATO ministerial communiqué, 15 December 1966](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/1966/12/15/final-communique); [NPG history](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/organization/nato-structure/nuclear-planning-group-npg).

France was not militarily isolated from alliance planning. The August 1967 Ailleret-Lemnitzer exchange established procedures for possible French-force cooperation in a NATO contingency while preserving French national command and excluding the French nuclear force. The 1974 Valentin-Ferber agreement extended arrangements to land forces, including the First Army. NATO's subsequently released 1988 French explanation says French forces could operate only as a group under national command, could be placed under an allied commander's operational control for a specific mission if the French government chose to commit them, and had no automatic peacetime assignment. It explicitly states that the nuclear aspect was excluded: [NATO Archives, C-R(88)16, annex](https://archives.nato.int/uploads/r/nato-archives-online/a/c/0/ac021e011a51a3fb1602cd14a08cebb1a9cfa4156225d02393289ccba5635c48/C-R_88_16_ENG_NHQL504583.pdf); [NATO Archives catalogue, Ailleret-Lemnitzer exchange](https://archives.nato.int/exchange-of-letters-between-saceur-and-the-french-commander-in-chief-concerning-cooperation-with-french-forces-in-germany-lemnitzer-ailleret-agreement/informationobject/reports).

French nuclear autonomy was a foundational condition, not a side effect of leaving command. The concept should not imply that rejoining command automatically transfers control of the force de frappe, or that France enters allied nuclear sharing. France remained outside the NPG throughout this period and later explicitly said it was not party to NATO's 1979 dual-track decision: [NATO statement, 11 June 1987](https://www.nato.int/fr/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/1987/06/11/statement). A 1988 Mitterrand speech reaffirmed both France's Article 5 commitment and its distinct position on integrated command: [Élysée transcript, 2 March 1988](https://www.elysee.fr/francois-mitterrand/1988/03/02/declaration-de-m-francois-mitterrand-president-de-la-republique-lors-de-la-seance-douverture-du-sommet-de-lotan-sur-la-dissuasion-nucleaire-et-le-desarmement-bruxelles-mercredi-2-mars-1988).

## Credible policy directions and trade-offs

These are concept recommendations, not claims that the alternatives occurred. Keep the main outcomes mutually exclusive; let players choose practical instruments inside each.

1. **Gaullist autonomy within the Alliance.** Preserve treaty membership, political consultation and Article 5 solidarity while keeping French forces and nuclear planning outside integrated command. Develop the existing Ailleret-Lemnitzer model; from 1974, Valentin-Ferber provides a real precedent for extending conditional land-force coordination. Its trade-off is continued French discretion and nuclear separation at the cost of routine access to integrated defence planning and allied command structures. This is the strongest January 1970 baseline.

2. **Selective Atlantic rapprochement.** Negotiate additional, explicitly conditional force-cooperation and interoperability arrangements while retaining the treaty and national command. The 1967 and 1974 accords are actual precedents, not hypothetical new NATO membership. A later-stage branch could explore deeper political and operational participation, but should present full integrated-command return before 2009 as an alternate departure from the historical trajectory. The historical move toward closer cooperation was gradual under Mitterrand in the late 1980s and 1990s; France formally resumed full participation in 2009. Trade-off: greater allied planning influence and coordination, with pressure to accept more routine planning obligations and constrain unilateral freedom. Preserve autonomous nuclear command as a condition unless the player selects a distinctly radical alternate.

3. **European defence pillar.** Pursue a stronger European contribution and bilateral coordination within the Atlantic alliance, with French nuclear autonomy and separate command maintained. The 1974 Ottawa Declaration gives contemporary language for European allies taking responsibility for common defence while affirming indispensable North American forces and close consultation: [Ottawa Declaration on Atlantic Relations, 19 June 1974](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/1974/06/19/declaration-on-atlantic-relations). The Franco-German Defence and Security Council was established in 1988, and the Franco-German Brigade was founded in 1989, after Mitterrand-Kohl cooperation; they are later precedents, not institutions available in 1970: [French Ministry of Armed Forces on the bilateral council](https://www.defense.gouv.fr/dgris/espace-euratlantique/grands-partenaires/allemagne); [French Ministry of Armed Forces on the brigade](https://www.defense.gouv.fr/terre/unites-larmee-terre/nos-brigades/brigade-franco-allemande-bfa/brigade-franco-allemande). Trade-off: more European strategic capacity and shared planning with West Germany, but negotiations, capability coordination and the risk of friction with allies concerned that a European pillar might weaken Atlantic cohesion.

The 1979 double-track decision and 1983 missile deployments can provide a late-era crisis fork around arms-control diplomacy and alliance consultation. They should not be presented as a 1970 choice, nor as a French deployment obligation: the 1979 decision was made by NATO, but France later stated it was not party to it. NATO's chronology describes the decision as a linked modernization and arms-control track: [NATO chronology, 1979](https://www.nato.int/docu/update/70-79/1979e.htm); [French statement, 1987](https://www.nato.int/fr/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/1987/06/11/statement).

## Treaty denunciation: legal option, radical alternate

Article 13 permits any party to cease being a party one year after giving notice of denunciation to the United States government, once the treaty has been in force for twenty years. Since the treaty entered into force on 24 August 1949, that clause was available by 1970. A notice in 1970 would therefore mean withdrawal from treaty membership only after a one-year interval; it would end France's treaty obligations, including Article 5, unlike the 1966 command withdrawal. The treaty text is explicit: [North Atlantic Treaty, Article 13](https://www.nato.int/docu/basictxt/treaty.htm).

This is legally credible but historically a radical Gaullist alternate, not the natural continuation of de Gaulle's 1966 decision. De Gaulle chose to retain alliance membership and political participation while changing command status. If included, make denunciation a late, high-commitment branch requiring a conspicuous diplomatic break, with explicit loss of collective-defence treaty membership; do not portray it as a routine “complete the 1966 withdrawal” step or as having occurred historically.

## Naming and dating guardrails

- Call the 1966 act “withdrawal from NATO's integrated military command,” not withdrawal from NATO.
- Use **NATO political membership / Article 5**, **integrated military command**, **NPG nuclear planning**, and **national command** as separate concepts and effects.
- Date Ailleret-Lemnitzer to 1967 and Valentin-Ferber to 1974. Show them as existing arrangements to implement, expand, renegotiate or reject, not as new NATO accession agreements.
- Date the Ottawa Declaration to 1974; the NATO double-track decision to 1979; Mitterrand's public affirmation of Article 5 alongside separate command status to 1988; the Franco-German Defence and Security Council to 1988; the Franco-German Brigade to 1989; and full integrated-command return to 2009.
- Do not place France in the NPG, DPC or NATO integrated force assignments at the 1970 start. Do not make it party to the 1979 double-track decision. Do not treat French nuclear forces as part of NATO nuclear sharing.
