# Domestic security measures: historical basis and gameplay interpretation

<!-- IMPERIA CW — Cold War: historical precedents for the IRA and RAF cabinet measures. -->

This is a policy model for multiplayer roleplay, not a reconstruction of individual operations. Amounts, percentage rolls, durations, recruitment changes and numeric thresholds are **authored gameplay values**, not measured historical causal effects or historical budgets. All monetary commitments use the mod's weekly billion-dollar account. Cash grants are separate upfront payments. There is no conversion of a historical annual budget in this feature.

An independence party, an occupied population and a clandestine armed organisation are not interchangeable. These measures are limited to the registered British IRA and German RAF cases. Colonial administrations retain their own policy interface. Community tension represents civilian grievances and institutional legitimacy; it is distinct from IRA membership or approval of violence. This remains a simplified regional model: it does not simulate the full range of nationalist, unionist, republican and loyalist organisations.

## Northern Ireland

- **Emergency internment and review of detention.** Detention without trial was introduced on 9 August 1971. Contemporary parliamentary criticism explicitly connects executive detention powers to minority disaffection. The official contemporaneous announcement is recorded in the US diplomatic archive. The gameplay measure therefore removes some immediate organisation strength but raises community tension, carries a local manpower restriction and adds recruitment pressure while detention remains active. Reviewing detention removes its recurring appropriation and restriction, but returning detainees restore some organisational capacity. This is a strategic abstraction, not a statement that all detainees were IRA members. Sources: [Hansard, 29 November 1971](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1971/nov/29/northern-ireland); [FRUS memorandum, 13 August 1971](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v41/d346).

- **Fair employment and complaints.** The Fair Employment (Northern Ireland) Act 1976 established an agency to promote equal employment opportunity and address religious/political discrimination. A parliamentary committee records the preceding 1973 working party and the development of the legislation. The measure combines employment review and complaints administration as an institutional policy option: it reduces grievances over time, costs money and political capital, and cannot coexist with a newly adopted internment policy. It does not instantly arrest people or dissolve the IRA. Sources: [1976 Act, original text](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1976/25/enacted); [Northern Ireland Affairs Committee, origins of the Act](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm199899/cmselect/cmniaf/95/9505.htm).

- **Evidence-led casework.** Criminal investigation is represented separately from blanket executive detention. The contemporary Compton inquiry describes ordinary prosecution alongside detention. The game's paid casework programme modestly reduces organisation strength and continues to consume a weekly appropriation; it is not an infallible intelligence or arrest mechanism. Source: [Compton Report, 1971, paragraphs 8–10](https://cain.ulster.ac.uk/hmso/compton.htm), an official report hosted by Ulster University.

- **Confidential contacts and a provisional ceasefire.** The Irish National Archives preserves June 1972 statements concerning possible British-government/IRA talks. The contemporary record supports a contact option; it does not justify guaranteed agreement or permanent peace. The game therefore rolls for a temporary cessation or failed talks, retains recruitment and foreign assistance, and allows spoiler incidents even during a cessation. Source: [Irish Department of Foreign Affairs file, 13–14 June 1972](https://cain.ulster.ac.uk/nai/1972/nai_DFA-2003-17-300_1972-06-13.pdf).

- **Constitutional power-sharing talks and obstruction.** Sunningdale involved the British and Irish governments and constitutional parties; it was not an IRA disarmament pact. The 1973 agreement led to an executive in January 1974 which collapsed in May after anti-agreement mobilisation and a general strike. The game consequently requires a quieter environment for constitutional talks, imposes political costs, and schedules an obstruction risk. It never treats constitutional participation as equivalent to IRA surrender. Sources: [December 1973 agreement text, UN Peacemaker](https://peacemaker.un.org/sites/default/files/document/files/2024/05/suningdale20agreement.pdf); [UK Parliament, Sunningdale](https://www.parliament.uk/about/living-heritage/evolutionofparliament/legislativescrutiny/parliamentandireland/overview/sunningdale-agreement/).

The player may pursue these institutional alternatives earlier or later in an alternate-history campaign when their situational prerequisites hold. They are not automatically enacted on their historical dates. In particular, no successful contact roll automatically removes the organisation or manufactures an independence agreement.

## Federal Republic of Germany

- **Federal-state case coordination.** A contemporary BKA publication describes INPOL as a linked federal/state police information system following the interior ministers' 1972 decision and subsequent statutory underpinning. The measure funds coordination and improves existing wanted-person traces; it does not produce automatic arrests or retroactively claim that a particular historical arrest resulted from a database match. Source: [BKA conference publication, Polizei und Justiz, 1976, section 3.2](https://www.bka.de/SharedDocs/Downloads/DE/Publikationen/Herbsttagungen/Tagungen1954Bis2001/1976_PolizeiUndJustiz.pdf?__blob=publicationFile&v=1).

- **Judicial scrutiny and prison medical review.** Restrictions on defendants, detention conditions, hunger strikes and government responses were contested institutions of the period. The Bundestag debated the state's position on compulsory feeding in December 1974. The two game measures offer a more restrained alternative: improve rule of law and reduce milieu/hunger pressure at the cost of security capacity and paid supervision. **These are authored institutional alternatives, not claims that a programme with the displayed name was historically implemented, or that medical review guarantees an end to a hunger strike.** Sources: [Bundestag plenary record, 5 December 1974, p. 9106](https://dserver.bundestag.de/btp/07/07134.pdf); [Federal Agency for Civic Education, legislative responses](https://www.bpb.de/themen/linksextremismus/geschichte-der-raf/49232/reaktionen-des-staates/).

- **Wanted notices, computer dragnet, crown witnesses and exit offers.** These are existing German-module actions exposed through the common cabinet. The adapter preserves their native prerequisites, political costs, effects and cooldowns. It does not add a second implementation. Later institutional developments must not be read as already-established 1970 policy: their native provisions still need to be unlocked, which can represent alternate legislative choices. The Bundesarchiv records the 1990 exposure and prosecution of RAF members sheltered in the GDR; this is context for later witness/exit issues, not evidence that the FRG administered the GDR's shelter operation. Source: [Bundesarchiv educational source material on RAF and Stasi](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/assets/bundesarchiv/de/Bildungsmaterialien/Begleitmaterial_anarcho-terroristische_Kraefte.pdf).

## Runtime boundaries

The new programme costs stop at expiry or loss of the relevant domestic case. Cooldowns remain, preventing cancellation/reconquest exploits. National and regional payment guards are rechecked at activation. Foreign aid is reconciled before evaluating power-sharing eligibility. Neither reform nor coordination cancels another player's existing foreign deliveries. Only the existing German module handles arrests, wanted-person status, attack planning, prison regimes and hostage outcomes.
