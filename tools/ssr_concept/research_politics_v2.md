IMPERIA COLD WAR — independent-party programme redesign research

# Political programme redesign for the 1970 SSR concept

## Direction

The current `party_modules()` branches are still political templates. `communist`, `liberal`, `social`, and the fallback branch each stamp nearly the same constitutional, agricultural, industrial, and executive choices onto different countries. Renaming those cards or adding one country-specific finale does not make the policy programme belong to that party. The prose also slips into author notes such as “registered organisation,” “other RP countries,” “does not grant,” and explanations of what the focus does not do. Those sentences do not belong in player-facing descriptions.

Rebuild each selected slot as a country-and-organization route. Treat the real identity of the organization, its relationship to Soviet power, and the economic institutions it could command as the inputs. Preserve exact-slot plus actual-sovereignty completion and activation guards; leave every route visible. Do not require a prior SSR focus. The common independent tree can retain civil administration, public accounts, supply continuity, and recovery from separation. Put ownership, labour, investment, trade, and the pace of market reform inside each party's route.

Every route should contain at least one consequential economic choice and meaningful follow-through. A rapid price and ownership liberalisation route is a valid alternative, but it needs a clearly named advantage such as faster enterprise conversion or private investment, and a clear price such as lost employment, weaker public services, inflation exposure, or political instability. The gradual alternative should also provide a tangible benefit and pay a real cost in slower investment, lower short-term output, or a heavier fiscal burden. Tune actual numbers separately against the 1970 start and existing MD effects.

## Data shape

Replace the family switch with party-specific route records. A useful concept shape is:

```text
country tag and exact party slot
party name as registered in the 1970 roster
identity: party, exile institution, underground body, or alternate governing coalition
1970 status: operating in the homeland, operating in exile, or not yet formed
historical political aims and relationship to Soviet power
counterfactual premise for governing the independent republic
economic doctrine: ownership, labour, agriculture/industry, external capital, reform speed
route nodes: stable ID, title, finished English description, grouped prerequisites,
             exclusive choices, country-specific reward intent, and exact party gate
later analogues: named with dates and described as later, never backdated into 1970
```

For an organization that is an exile government, cultural circle, trade union, writers' union, or local institution rather than a party, say so plainly in internal design data. If it is selected as an independent governing coalition, state the counterfactual coalition premise there. Do not explain that premise in focus prose with phrases like “a registered circle becomes a coalition” or “other RP countries.” Instead write the actual political story: who is negotiating with whom, which institution holds authority, and which social group bears the cost.

The independent route root and every party focus need both the actual independent-state status and the exact slot's native `*_are_in_power` check. Keep future focus cards inspectable regardless of government. A sovereign state that never completed SSR focuses must still be able to start its chosen party route immediately. Never reuse the SSR secretariat's First Secretary leadership story as the independent republic's political identity.

## January 1970 versus later history

The campaign opens in January 1970. The Soviet constitutional republics are not independent parliamentary systems, and most have no legal opposition parties. In the counterfactual, a Soviet breakup or accepted secession lets an actual listed party or institution govern a new state. That counterfactual authority is the design premise, not evidence that a later independence movement was already active in 1970.

Keep these dates distinct in the content:

- Baltic Popular Fronts, Sajudis, Rahvarinne and comparable mass movements are late-1980s developments. In 1970, the available alternatives are exile governments and parties, surviving cultural organizations, or explicitly counterfactual coalitions. Lithuania's *Chronicle of the Catholic Church* begins in 1972; do not write it as a 1970 institution.
- Georgia's language demonstrations occurred in April 1978. They can be an inherited national memory or a later inspiration, not an event already fought in January 1970.
- Birlik formed in Uzbekistan in 1989 and Erk split from it in 1990. Agzybirlik in Turkmenistan is a 1989 movement. The Democratic Movement of Kyrgyzstan was a 1990 movement. These are later analogues, not 1970 party organizations.
- The Alash movement and party belong to the 1917-1920 period. Later groups such as Azat drew on its memory; they are not an uninterrupted clandestine party in 1970.
- The Kazakh Nevada-Semipalatinsk movement dates to 1989. *Earth, Bow to Man!* can inspire a counterfactual environmental caucus but is not a 1970 organisation.
- In Tajikistan, an underground religious revival network developed in the 1970s and became publicly political in the late 1980s; the all-Union Islamic Renaissance Party was founded in 1990 and the Tajik party was registered in 1991. Slot 12 can only be a plausible counterfactual civic religious coalition in this start, not a formal 1970 IRP.
- Moldova had a real clandestine National Patriotic Front founded in 1969. The late-1980s Popular Front and interethnic counter-movements are later and separate. The Front's recorded programme sought independence and subsequent union with socialist Romania. A game route may model its independence phase; unification requires a distinct Romanian agreement and should not appear as a free focus reward.
- In Ukraine, the UPR State Centre in exile and its National Council operated through the exile period. OUN-M and OUN-B were distinct exile factions. Their rivalry and political methods must remain distinct. The Sixtiers were a cultural and human-rights generation, not a 1970 political party. They can only lead through a counterfactual coalition of dissident intellectuals and civic institutions.

## Four fully written route samples

Each sample has 13 focuses. The descriptions are player-facing English drafts; bracketed labels identify a mutually exclusive design choice and are not part of localization. The one-time rewards, costs, and signed modifier amounts remain to be calibrated against the 1970 economy. Every branch is visible and uses the exact governing slot only when completed.

### OUN-M: a conservative coalition, not a Bandera state

The Melnyk wing operated in exile and remained distinct from the revolutionary Bandera wing. Its Christian-national language, older exile cadre, and participation in Ukrainian exile institutions support a conservative national coalition route. It can oppose Soviet domination while choosing parliamentary accommodation over a single revolutionary command. This distinction is supported by the separate OUN factions and the postwar exile parties' coalition activity.[Harvard Ukrainian Studies](https://husj.harvard.edu/articles/the-nation-in-a-nutshell-ukrainian-displaced-persons-camps-in-postwar-germany), [2024 study of Ukrainian parties in exile](https://elibrary.kubg.edu.ua/id/eprint/51066/)

| Focus | Description |
|---|---|
| The Melnyk Council | The older exile leadership has carried the demand for Ukrainian independence through decades of division. Its first task is to turn disciplined national solidarity into authority answerable to a Ukrainian public. |
| Christian Civic Charter | Parish, school, veterans' and cultural societies give the new state a civic foundation. The constitution must protect the freedom of conscience that Soviet rule denied. |
| A National Council | The Ukrainian National Council has brought rival exile parties into one parliament. The Melnyk wing gains influence by sharing the state with those who do not share its creed. |
| Language and Schooling | Ukrainian-language schools and universities can restore a public culture long pushed to the margins. Teachers and local councils need resources before the first class opens. |
| Cooperative Countryside | **Exclusive choice A.** Farm cooperatives join small producers to rural credit, storage, and machinery. Their gradual growth protects smallholders but leaves capital-intensive investment slower. |
| Family Holdings | **Exclusive choice B.** Secure titles and private farm credit reward productive owners and invite investment. Land concentration and the exclusion of tenants become harder to prevent. |
| Industrial Credit Bank | Republican credit directs savings toward machine tools, food processing, and power repairs. Lending rules restrain political favourites but slow the first wave of construction. |
| Public Works Contracts | Local firms compete to rebuild rail, housing, and farm infrastructure. Faster contracting stimulates demand but makes public oversight more difficult. |
| Orderly Currency Reform | **Exclusive choice A.** A staged currency and price transition protects wages and pension payments. The treasury carries the cost while old prices and losses are unwound. |
| Immediate Price Liberalisation | **Exclusive choice B.** Free prices expose shortages and attract private goods quickly. Household savings and state enterprises face a severe shock, and the government risks a legitimacy crisis. |
| A Civilian Defence Statute | The armed forces answer to law and elected ministers. Veterans receive a place in the state, but command cannot rest on a party's wartime claim alone. |
| The Cooperative Compact | Parliament bargains with farm, industrial, and professional associations over the costs of reconstruction. Rural producers gain a voice while national investment decisions take longer. |
| A Parliamentary Republic | The State Centre's inheritance becomes a republic shared with parties that fought for independence by different means. Its authority will rest on elections, law, and the consent of citizens at home. |

The economic fork is not “good market versus bad market.” Cooperatives and private holdings compete over who controls land and credit; staged monetary reform and immediate liberalisation compete over transition speed, household protection, and inflation risk. The common sovereign recovery system remains separate.

### OUN-B: revolutionary authority with an explicit political price

The Bandera wing claimed a revolutionary mandate and pursued a more centralized, authoritarian conception of national rule than OUN-M. Its anti-communism is central, but the branch should show the cost of one-party authority and its wartime legacy rather than turning militancy into free military strength. The postwar OUN-B's exclusion from permanent participation in the UNRada is a useful distinction from the broad exile parliament.[Harvard Ukrainian Studies](https://husj.harvard.edu/articles/the-nation-in-a-nutshell-ukrainian-displaced-persons-camps-in-postwar-germany)

| Focus | Description |
|---|---|
| A Revolutionary Mandate | The Bandera movement holds that Soviet rule can be overturned only by a sovereign Ukrainian state. The new government inherits both a determined cadre and a public wary of rule by one organization. |
| National Mobilisation | Cadres carry administration into the countryside, factories, and schools. Their discipline raises the state's reach, while their demand for loyalty narrows the space for rivals. |
| The National Assembly | **Exclusive choice A.** A constituent assembly admits parties, churches, unions, and regional delegates into a contested settlement. It limits the movement's command and makes policy slower. |
| A Unified National Front | **Exclusive choice B.** A single governing movement coordinates appointments and national campaigns. It can act quickly but turns dissent into a threat to the state. |
| Land for the Nation | Land claims are reviewed under one national statute, with priority for those who work the soil. The process weakens old estates but consumes time and provokes disputes over restitution. |
| National Credit | State-backed credit supports machine works and agricultural processing chosen for strategic value. Directed investment builds capacity but invites political allocation and inefficient plants. |
| Private Workshops | **Exclusive choice A.** Small firms receive secure contracts and room to hire. Consumer goods return sooner, while private wealth gains influence over the new republic. |
| A National Industrial Syndicate | **Exclusive choice B.** Industrial syndicates coordinate employers and workers under state priorities. The system can concentrate investment but excludes independent unions and competitors. |
| Gradual Currency Settlement | **Exclusive choice A.** Savings and wages are protected as prices and contracts change in stages. This preserves public trust but keeps unproductive enterprises alive longer. |
| Emergency Market Break | **Exclusive choice B.** Prices and ownership are changed at once to end shortages and attract capital. Output may rebound sooner, but savings, employment, and political support can collapse together. |
| The State and the Church | Religious communities regain public life under a national law. The state gains a source of solidarity but must resist turning a shared faith into a test of citizenship. |
| A National Service | Veterans and civic organizations receive a public role in reconstruction, supervised by civilian officials. Their experience is useful, but a large service apparatus consumes money and can intimidate opponents. |
| The Cadre Republic | The movement completes its revolutionary state through a strong executive and national command. Its ability to mobilise is formidable; its narrow political base remains a lasting weakness. |

The assembly and unified-front choices must have different real benefits: broad consent and outside expertise versus faster decisions and cadre coordination. Each also carries its own cost. Later OUN/UPA remembrance is not evidence of majority support in 1970 or a combat-reward justification.

### Armenia: Armenian Revolutionary Federation (ARF)

The ARF's exile organizations opposed Soviet rule and kept Armenian independence at the centre of their politics. Its socialist-national roots make a simple liberal-versus-authoritarian template inaccurate. The route should make diaspora solidarity, national institutions, public ownership, and the party's claim to represent Armenian memory compete with the need for a broad domestic settlement. Hunchak and Ramkavar positions toward Soviet Armenia differed markedly from the ARF's anti-Soviet line.[Diaspora party history](https://library.fes.de/libalt/journals/swetsfulltext/19480960.pdf), [National Library of Armenia research summary](https://etd.nla.am/7507/2/Kh.-Stepanyan-%D5%BD%D5%A5%D5%B4%D5%A1%D5%A3%D5%AB%D6%80.pdf)

| Focus | Description |
|---|---|
| The Federation's Return | The Dashnaktsutyun has carried the cause of Armenian independence across the diaspora. In Yerevan, the party must turn that claim into a government that citizens can join and challenge. |
| The Republic's Memory | Archives, schools, and public commemorations restore histories suppressed by Soviet rule. The national story must make room for families whose memories differ. |
| A National Assembly | **Exclusive choice A.** Seats are shared with other Armenian parties and community institutions. Coalition rule broadens legitimacy but gives rivals a say over the national agenda. |
| The Federation's Executive | **Exclusive choice B.** A strong cabinet acts on security and reconstruction through the party's disciplined network. Decisions are faster, while opposition fears another monopoly of power. |
| Diaspora Development Bank | Diaspora subscriptions finance engineering workshops, utilities, and technical education. Capital and expertise arrive with outside influence over which projects take priority. |
| Public Works Authority | **Exclusive choice A.** The state develops power, transport, and engineering through public enterprises. It preserves national control but asks the treasury to carry long-term investment. |
| Armenian Enterprise Charters | **Exclusive choice B.** Private and cooperative firms gain rights to import machinery and sell beyond the state plan. Production can adapt quickly, but strategic sectors become harder to direct. |
| Engineers of the Republic | Universities and research institutes link skilled graduates to industrial maintenance and new production. The investment serves long-term capacity rather than immediate household supply. |
| Rural Credit and Roads | Rural banks and transport links give growers and small workshops access to markets. The policy eases regional dependence but diverts capital from major urban industry. |
| Protected Price Transition | **Exclusive choice A.** Essential prices change in stages while wages and pensions retain public support. The social peace costs money and prolongs shortages. |
| Immediate Market Release | **Exclusive choice B.** Price controls and enterprise privileges end rapidly, opening trade and investment. Goods move sooner, but savings, employment, and the party's social base take the shock. |
| Schools of the Armenian World | Schools and cultural institutes connect the republic to Armenian communities abroad. They strengthen language and technical learning, while the state must guard against a single political authority claiming the diaspora. |
| A Social Republic | The federation binds national independence to public responsibility for work, education, and care. The state draws on an international Armenian network, but its promises will be judged by citizens who stayed through Soviet rule. |

The choice between ARF and Hunchak must not be superficial. ARF begins from anti-Soviet independence and a diaspora organization with strong claims on national memory. Hunchak's route below begins from a socialist party that historically maintained warmer ties with Soviet Armenia and criticized Dashnak claims. Neither receives an automatic military or territorial reward.

### Armenia: Social Democrat Hunchakian Party

Hunchak is a socialist diaspora party, not a second Dashnak branch. Its history included support for Soviet Armenia and criticism of the ARF's claim to inherit the First Republic. In a 1970 independence counterfactual, its political challenge is to preserve Armenian statehood while persuading voters that public ownership and social services can be accountable without Soviet monopoly.[Diaspora party history](https://library.fes.de/libalt/journals/swetsfulltext/19480960.pdf), [historical overview](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/bell-and-banner-armenian-revolutionaries.htm)

| Focus | Description |
|---|---|
| A Workers' Republic | The Hunchakian tradition binds Armenian self-government to social equality. Independence now requires a public mandate broader than the party's old diaspora networks. |
| A Labour Congress | Trade unions and professional associations negotiate wages, safety, and enterprise duties. The settlement protects workers but slows decisions in factories already short of supplies. |
| The Civic Assembly | **Exclusive choice A.** A multiparty assembly reviews the cabinet and public accounts. It can broaden support beyond the workers' movement but weakens the party's control over its own reforms. |
| The Social Cabinet | **Exclusive choice B.** A disciplined cabinet coordinates state enterprises and social services. It moves faster but concentrates decisions among party officials and managers. |
| Public Enterprise Charter | State firms publish obligations, costs, and production goals. Public ownership survives, but managers can no longer hide every loss behind a plan target. |
| Workers' Councils | **Exclusive choice A.** Employees elect councils with authority over safety, training, and shop-floor organization. Workers gain a real voice while production targets become harder to change. |
| Professional Directors | **Exclusive choice B.** Technical managers receive clear production authority and rewards for reliable output. The factories move faster, but workers have less influence over restructuring. |
| Housing and Clinics | Public funds repair housing and local clinics before promising a wider welfare expansion. This protects daily life but uses capital that could modernize heavy industry. |
| Armenian Technical Schools | Engineering institutes train a new generation for utilities, machine works, and public services. The payback is gradual and depends on keeping skilled graduates at home. |
| Gradual Enterprise Reform | **Exclusive choice A.** Prices and contracts change in stages as public firms meet new accounts. Employment and household savings are protected, but the budget keeps supporting weak plants. |
| Emergency Price Release | **Exclusive choice B.** The cabinet ends price controls quickly and lets firms renegotiate contracts. Market supply can recover sooner, but wages, pensions, and public services lose their shield. |
| Diaspora Education Compact | Hunchak schools and cultural societies support Armenian education while recognizing Soviet Armenia's community ties. The programme opens channels for knowledge and investment but tests the party's authority over its own network. |
| Social Democratic Armenia | Parliamentary law, organized labour, and public ownership form a distinct Armenian settlement. The party gives up the claim that one revolutionary organization speaks for the entire nation. |

### Cross-route integrity

These four routes deliberately differ in their economic core: OUN-M's cooperatives and negotiated coalition; OUN-B's national command and authoritarian syndicates; ARF's anti-Soviet diaspora-backed development; and Hunchak's social ownership and labour bargaining. They should not share a generic “competitive markets / local enterprise” chain beneath different titles. Their shock or gradual reform choices should also be mechanically different enough that route completion can be felt in play.

The Ukrainian UPR State Centre and URDP routes still need equivalent party-specific design before implementation. The UPR route should centre constitutional exile continuity, a parliament of parties, land and property claims, and a social-market bargain. The URDP should centre a worker-farmer coalition, labour law, land redistribution, municipal services, and cooperative credit. The Sixtiers route should centre constitutional rights, language, independent courts and pluralism, and must keep its alternate 1970 coalition premise explicit in design data.
