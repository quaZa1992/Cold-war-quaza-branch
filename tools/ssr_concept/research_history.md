# Research notes: Union republics and an SSR transition concept

Scope: January 1970, fourteen non-RSFSR Union republics, with historical comparisons through the early independence period. This is research for a concept, not a claim that post-1980s organizations existed in 1970.

## Institutional facts that support a shared opening

For January 1970, use the 1936 USSR Constitution as amended, not the 1977 text. Articles 13-18 defined the Union as a federation of equal Union Republics; each republic had its own constitution, could exercise authority outside the Union's enumerated competences, and formally retained a right to secede. Amendments admitted the three Baltic republics and the Moldavian SSR in 1940. The 1936 text also distinguished autonomous republics inside a Union Republic, with their own constitutions and republican-level Supreme Soviets and governments (Articles 22-29b and 89-93). That establishes the contemporary basis for shared SSR status while preserving the distinction between a Union Republic and an ASSR. [1936 USSR Constitution, as amended](https://seventeenmoments.web.illinois.edu/1936-2/stalin-constitution/stalin-constitution-texts/the-stalin-constitution/); [UN Yearbook 1947, constitutional amendments](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/860913/files/1947-E.pdf)

The 1936 Constitution reserved to the Union questions of war and peace, organization of USSR defense and direction of all USSR armed forces, state security, foreign trade, the national economic plan, monetary and credit policy, transport, and all-Union industries. Republics nevertheless had republican governments, budgets and plans, internal affairs, state security organs, and limited domestic administrative responsibilities. The formal state offices were the Supreme Soviet and its collective Presidium, chaired by a republican Presidium chairman, plus the Council of Ministers and its chairman. The First Secretary was party chief and in practice the dominant political leader, but was not automatically the formal head of state or republican premier. [1936 Constitution, Articles 14-15, 57-63, 74-88](https://seventeenmoments.web.illinois.edu/1936-2/stalin-constitution/stalin-constitution-texts/the-stalin-constitution/)

Military nuance: the 1 February 1944 amendment added a formal right for each Union Republic to have republican military formations and allowed a republican defense commissariat. That was a constitutional possibility on paper, not a functioning system of sovereign republican armies. The new right was not implemented: the wartime national formations were dissolved after the war, and no republic established a defense ministry or appointed a defense minister. The same amendment left the Union in charge of the organization and command of all Soviet armed forces. Thus the January 1970 branch can credibly cover domestic military preparation, conscription, civil defense, military training, recruitment and industrial support, but its armed forces should remain part of Union command until independence. [1944 amendment text](https://www.openstax.marxists.info/history/ussr/government/constitution/1944/1944-amendments.htm); [M. H. Van den Heuvel, “The Union Republics and Soviet Diplomacy”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/union-republics-and-soviet-diplomacy-concepts-institutions-and-practices/51F4F79FBAC93E77F07E0C71B83412B4); [U.S. diplomatic memo, 3 February 1944](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1944v04/d755)

The 1977 Constitution, cited below only as a later comparison, retained the same distinction between Union Republics and Autonomous Republics and gives an easily accessible later list of autonomous territories. Do not present its wording or article numbering as the January 1970 constitutional text. [1977 USSR Constitution](https://www.departments.bucknell.edu/Russian/const/77cons03.html)

The First Secretary was the top party official and, in practice, the dominant republican political leader. That office was distinct from the formal state offices: each SSR had a Presidium of its Supreme Soviet, chaired by a formal head of state, and a Council of Ministers with a chair. The party leader often also held state or Union positions, but the names and posts must not be conflated in country history. Republican secretaries were central figures in elite recruitment and bargaining with Moscow. [Rigby, “The Soviet Regional Leadership: The Brezhnev Generation”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/slavic-review/article/soviet-regional-leadership-the-brezhnev-generation/DA80BDA923FC6CAFE42842C27DEE5247); [Encyclopedia of Ukraine, Petro Shelest](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CS%5CH%5CShelestPetro.htm)

The roster demonstrates why a universal "First Secretary" career or elite-management mechanism is plausible, but an identical leader story is not. Long tenure was common, while the balance between locally rooted cadres, central oversight, corruption control, and locally tolerated economic discretion varied. In the Baltic republics, second secretaries were usually Moscow-linked supervisors; the exact pattern varied, with Estonia an exception for longer. Georgia's and Azerbaijan's 1969-72 cadre shakeups differed from Lithuania's locally entrenched Sniečkus network. ## Why the RSFSR is a separate design problem

The RSFSR is still one of the fifteen Union republics and must not be demoted to an ordinary oblast or treated as outside the Union-republic framework. Its scale and federal structure make a one-to-one port of a smaller SSR tree misleading. The 1936 Constitution, as amended, listed numerous ASSRs inside the RSFSR; by comparison, Georgia, Azerbaijan, Uzbekistan, and Tajikistan also had their own autonomous units. These are autonomy and center-periphery questions inside a Union republic, not fourteen additional Union republic equivalents. The later 1977 text is useful for a consolidated roster, but is not the 1970 source. [1936 USSR Constitution, Articles 22-29b and 89-93](https://seventeenmoments.web.illinois.edu/1936-2/stalin-constitution/stalin-constitution-texts/the-stalin-constitution/); [1977 Constitution, Articles 82-88](https://www.departments.bucknell.edu/Russian/const/77cons03.html)

Design inference: give the RSFSR its own scenario architecture for federal republics, autonomous territories, Union institutions, and the absence of a separate RSFSR Communist Party until 1990. The other fourteen share an SSR-status opening, though they should still have localized leader portraits, economic functions, politics, and transition programs. A single universal republic war or ethnic-fragmentation event would flatten these constitutional and regional differences.

## Common Soviet-era programme, with real regional variations

### Historical facts

All fourteen were deeply embedded in Union planning, Union defense command, centrally coordinated rail, energy, and payment systems, and inter-republic supply chains. Union status gave republican administrations a legal frame and representation, but Moscow retained key competences. The First Secretary bargained over targets, appointments, investment, and local discretion. Defense was a Union competence in practice and under the 1936 text; the 1944 republican-force clause remained unimplemented. A 1970 SSR military programme can emphasize domestic military preparation and training, civil defense, conscription and recruitment, industrial support, research, and strategic infrastructure under Union command. A separate sovereign army is appropriate only after an independence transition.

This common framework can support shared focuses on planning bargains, Union investment, heavy industry and defense supply, technical education, republic cadres, transport integration, and the political cost of dependence. The branches need not imply that every republic built the same factories or received equal returns.

### Region and country distinctions to preserve

- **Ukraine:** major heavy-industrial, coal, metallurgical, agricultural, and defense-industrial base. A republican planning bargain can increase output and skilled employment while risking consumer investment and dependence on Union orders. Shelest's actual limited Ukrainianization and demands for economic voice make a republican-interest path historically grounded, without turning him into an independence leader. [Encyclopedia of Ukraine](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CS%5CH%5CShelestPetro.htm)
- **Belarus:** industrial and agricultural integration, with an unusually durable party elite. Favor industrial throughput, transport, or local managerial discretion as real alternatives to an abstract national awakening. [Siegelbaum](https://seventeenmoments.web.illinois.edu/1973-2/shakeup-in-the-republics/)
- **Moldova:** agricultural specialization, food processing, and links to both Ukraine and Romania. Later identity politics involved language, script, and competing national projects; they should not be projected as a fixed January 1970 consensus.
- **Baltics:** more urbanized and industrialized than Central Asia, with dense links to the Soviet trade and energy system. Post-independence sources show the Baltic states had received over 90 percent of imports from the integrated Soviet structure and suffered when energy imports moved toward world prices. Avoid treating all three as equally energy-deficient: Estonia had domestic oil shale and exported electricity, while Latvia and Lithuania relied much more heavily on imported primary energy. These are retrospective vulnerability indicators, not proof of identical January 1970 shortages. [European Commission, Baltic macro-financial assistance](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/LexUriServ/LexUriServ.do?uri=COM%3A1994%3A0229%3AFIN%3AEN%3APDF); [Nordregio, Soviet energy inheritance](https://archive.nordregio.se/en/Metameny/About-Nordregio/Journal-of-Nordregio/2007/Journal-of-Nordregio-no-1-2007/The-Soviet-inheritance/index.html); [IMF Baltic stabilization history](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/book/9781557755193/ch01.xml)
- **South Caucasus:** later supply records show very different starting weaknesses. Armenia was highly interdependent with Union supply chains, exporting electronics, machinery and light industry while importing energy, food and industrial inputs; post-independence blockades and disrupted Georgian transit made the landlocked position acute. Georgia was a net energy importer in 1990, with roughly 80 percent of available energy resources imported, while its hydropower and supply routes were vulnerable to weather and conflict. Azerbaijan's mature oil sector and emerging Caspian development offered a different asset base, but its 1970s oil expansion still depended on technology, investment, and Union channels. Each also had distinct autonomous-territory and interethnic politics: Abkhazia, Adjara, and South Ossetia in Georgia; Nagorno-Karabakh and Nakhichevan in Azerbaijan. These were not interchangeable, and the 1970 tree should not presume later wars. [World Bank, Armenia economic report](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/442631511185640595/pdf/multi0page.pdf); [World Bank, Georgia energy situation](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/769171468749968800/pdf/multi0page.pdf); [Azerbaijan Ministry of Energy, oil industry history](https://minenergy.gov.az/en/neft/neft-senayesinin-inkisaf-tarixi); [1936 Constitution, territorial articles](https://seventeenmoments.web.illinois.edu/1936-2/stalin-constitution/stalin-constitution-texts/the-stalin-constitution/)
- **Kazakhstan:** vast territory, mining and metallurgy, agriculture, energy, space and nuclear testing legacies, and a highly mixed population. Its republic leadership faced an unusually broad territory and demographic balance. A resource and industrial autonomy route has a different cost profile from a small republic's.
- **Central Asia:** cotton and irrigation were central, but the water system also linked countries' energy security. Soviet-era arrangements moved water from Kyrgyz and Tajik mountain reservoirs downstream to irrigate Uzbekistan, Kazakhstan, and Turkmenistan; upstream republics received downstream fossil fuel and electricity in winter. The shared system helped meet plan targets and created mutual dependence. After independence, separate energy and water needs made it difficult to coordinate. Uzbekistan's cotton specialization created output and export earnings but also exposure to centrally set crop targets, water use, and ecological harm. [USDA Economic Research Service, Cotton and Hydropower in Central Asia](https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/43704/34671_eib_106.pdf?v=46571); [ESCAP, Water security in Central Asia and the Caucasus](https://www.unescap.org/sites/default/files/publications/WP_18_01_Water%20security%20in%20Central%20Asia%20and%20the%20Caucasus_.pdf); [IMF, Energy-Water Nexus](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781589063211/ch11.xml)
- **Turkmenistan:** natural gas, cotton, irrigation, desert infrastructure, and balancing of regional elite networks. Gapurov's 1969 appointment illustrates both Moscow's role and local elite balancing, not a simple autonomous tribal government. It later benefited as an energy exporter in the post-Soviet price transition, so it should not share an importer profile. [Cahiers d'Asie centrale](https://journals.openedition.org/asiecentrale/pdf/3227); [World Bank interstate terms-of-trade table](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/165461468770724398/pdf/multi0page.pdf)
- **Kyrgyzstan and Tajikistan:** mountainous, landlocked economies with hydropower, irrigation, mining, and regional transport constraints. Water, electricity, agriculture, and access corridors are more central than an oil export route. Both upstream republics were tied to downstream fuel and irrigation arrangements under the Soviet system. Later political pluralism and instability had different national and regional trajectories. [USDA Economic Research Service](https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/43704/34671_eib_106.pdf?v=46571); [OECD, Central Asia Since 1991](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/central-asia-since-1991_738202560358.html)

## Independence shock and policy choices

### Evidence-based problems

Independence did not begin from a clean national market. Before dissolution, inter-republic trade represented roughly 70-75 percent of former republics' total trade; production chains were specialized and transport networks crossed new borders. Afterward, trade and payment systems broke down, energy prices changed, transfers ended, and firms lacked market institutions. IMF analysis links the output collapse to intertwined supply, demand, trade, money, and institutional shocks, not to one universal cause. [IMF, The Disintegration of the Former Soviet Union: Economic Shock and Policy Response](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/IMF084/05300-9781557755186/05300-9781557755186/ch02.xml); [IMF, Russian Federation in Transition](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/1993/074/article-A001-en.xml); [Suesse, “Breaking the Unbreakable Union” summary](https://res.org.uk/mediabriefing/the-soviet-economic-collapse-new-evidence-of-the-potentially-harmful-consequences-of-state-break-up/)

Outcomes varied. Between 1990 and 1996 intra-ex-Soviet trade shares fell by different amounts; the cited study reports the smallest drops in Belarus and Moldova, and very steep drops in Estonia and Armenia. The Baltics had especially sharp energy terms-of-trade shocks. Central Asian states pursued different transition and political paths, while later growth outcomes were also shaped by differences in resource endowments and policy. [Springer, post-Soviet trade study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10644-020-09302-8); [IMF Baltic stabilization history](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/book/9781557755193/ch01.xml); [OECD, Central Asia Since 1991](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/central-asia-since-1991_738202560358.html)

### Alternate-history interpretation

An earlier or abrupt break in 1970 plausibly creates a crisis in contracts, currency, inter-republic deliveries, fuel, and the legal authority of plants and ministries. The exact severity is counterfactual: historical 1991 data should calibrate direction and mechanisms, not dictate identical scripted losses for every country. The Soviet plan system was already imperfect and informal local networks mattered; independence could also let governments redirect resources, negotiate bilateral supply pacts, or keep production running. No republic is doomed to permanent collapse.

### Distinct policy alternatives

| Policy | Benefit | Cost or risk | Strongest fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preserve inter-republic contracts and payments | Keeps inputs, energy, rail, and markets moving; avoids abrupt factory stoppages | Leaves leverage with Moscow and counterparties; accepts continued price and currency exposure | Belarus, Ukraine, Moldova, Kazakhstan, Central Asian republics |
| Rapid sovereignty, currency, and market conversion | Establishes independent fiscal/monetary authority and lets firms seek new markets | Short-term inflation, output loss, broken old supply links, and painful privatization | Especially plausible Baltic route; a costly option elsewhere |
| Stabilization with social guarantees | Protects wages, food, pensions, and strategic employers during conversion | Requires taxation/credit and delays price correction; risk of shortages and fiscal strain | Belarus, Ukraine, Moldova, Tajikistan, Kyrgyzstan |
| Resource sovereignty and export infrastructure | Captures resource revenue, builds external links, increases bargaining power | Infrastructure lead time, price volatility, and enclave/elite capture risk | Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan; later Uzbekistan |
| Diversification and local processing | Reduces dependence on a single crop, commodity, or buyer | Lower immediate export receipts and high investment needs | Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Moldova, Ukraine, Kazakhstan |
| Negotiated security and autonomy settlement | Can preserve corridors and reduce risks to minority regions | Concessions, veto points, and slow bargaining | Georgia, Azerbaijan, Armenia, Moldova, Kazakhstan |

These are attractive, conflicting approaches rather than deterministic national destinies. Avoid assigning all republics an automatic civil war, universal separatist revolt, or identical “post-Soviet collapse” debuff. Model common transition problems with country-specific intensity and let player choices change them. In particular, the later Tajik civil war, Nagorno-Karabakh war, Georgian conflicts, Moldova's Transnistria conflict, and Kyrgyz interethnic violence had particular actors and trajectories; they are not generic SSR independence outcomes. The 1970 alternate history can set up unresolved bargaining and institutional weaknesses without scripting those wars as inevitable.

## Design implications for the parent concept

1. Keep a common pre-independence SSR spine: Union plan bargaining, local cadres, strategic industry and military supply, technical education, transport, and limited republican discretion. Make individual effects reflect the actual local economy and leader.
2. Make First Secretary variation an institutional choice: central compliance and investment access, locally negotiated autonomy, or anti-corruption renewal with factional disruption. The history supports both the value and danger of long-serving patronage networks.
3. On independence, apply a common supply, payment, and budget transition problem, but vary intensity by country and let the player choose between keeping old links, fast conversion, social stabilization, or commodity-specific development.
4. Build party programmes from country-specific institutional successors. Later fronts prove that political alternatives arose, but do not put the 1988-91 organizations on a January 1970 roster unless the alternate history explicitly says they formed early.
5. Treat autonomy and security negotiations as country-specific, especially in Georgia, Azerbaijan, Armenia, and Moldova. A common menu can exist; its risks and prospective gains should reflect different territories and actors.
6. Keep Islamic political currents available only where historical parties and constituencies justify them, notably as one of several later Tajik alternatives and in distinct Uzbekistan/Central Asian contexts. No republic needs Shia or Salafi branches merely to fill an outlook grid.

## Evidence, inference, and balance labels

- **Historical facts:** officeholders, constitutional tiers, Soviet competences, actual specializations, trade integration, and later party formation dates cited above.
- **Alternate-history inference:** independence in 1970, what later organizations' predecessors might look like, and the pace or outcome of transition policy.
- **Balance proposals:** the shared temporary disruption, its country-specific severity, focus reward sizes, political access gates, recurring costs, and recovery speed. Calibrate against the mod's actual economy; the historical sources establish direction and trade-offs, not HOI4 numbers.
