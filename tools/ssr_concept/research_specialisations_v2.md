# Research note: Soviet republican production specializations and transition shock

## Scope and reading key

This is a historical menu for the 14 non-RSFSR Union republics in the January 1970 scenario. It distinguishes documented facilities in 1970 to 1990 from possible alternate development. **Historical fact** means a documented plant, resource, product, or policy. **Alternate-history inference** means a plausible focus direction built from that base. **Balance proposal** means a game effect recommendation, not a measured historical outcome.

The 1936 USSR Constitution, as amended by 1970, treated Union republics as constituent republics with their own constitutions and a formal right to secede. The Union retained war and peace, all-Union armed forces command, defense, security, national economic planning, transport, and all-Union enterprises. Republic sovereignty operated outside those reserved fields, while Union law prevailed on conflict. Autonomous republics were constitutionally within a Union republic, not equal Union constituents. Cite the 1936 text, especially Articles 13 to 20 and Chapter VII on autonomous republics: [University of Illinois, 1936 Constitution](https://seventeenmoments.web.illinois.edu/1936-2/stalin-constitution/stalin-constitution-texts/the-stalin-constitution/). Do not quote the 1977 Constitution as the law in 1970. A 1944 amendment added republican military formations in theory while leaving the Union with direction of all armed forces and authority to set organizational principles. Academic analysis reports the provision was never implemented after wartime national formations were dissolved; republic defense ministries were not established. This makes 1970 republican military work best represented by recruitment, military schools, reserve and civil-defense training, local logistics, and service within Union command, not a sovereign republican army. Sources: [1944 amendment text](https://openstax.marxists.info/history/ussr/government/constitution/1944/1944-amendments.htm); [Aspaturian, Union Republics and Soviet Diplomacy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/union-republics-and-soviet-diplomacy-concepts-institutions-and-practices/51F4F79FBAC93E77F07E0C71B83412B4); [UN Digital Library, Yearbook 1947](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/860913/files/1947-E.pdf).

## Sector guidance for a mutually exclusive tree menu

- **Aircraft and air systems:** strong historical fit for Ukraine, Georgia and Uzbekistan, but with different identities: Antonov design and aero engines in Ukraine, trainer and attack aircraft assembly in Georgia, heavy transports in Tashkent. Other SSRs can select aviation maintenance, air defense training or components only as alternate development unless an evidenced plant is established.
- **Armor and tanks:** Ukraine has the clearest tank-building precedent. Belarus's tractors and MZKT heavy chassis support military mobility and launch systems, not a tank factory. Do not grant a tank production branch to every SSR.
- **Artillery:** no broad republican artillery-gun sector is supported by the sources reviewed. SIPRI explicitly identifies Ukrainian gaps in tank guns and other artillery despite Ukraine's substantial military industry. Give all republics land-force training, recruitment, repair and logistics options; reserve gun manufacture for documented facilities or explicitly alternate investments. Belarus can be represented as heavy chassis and tractors for military mobility, not as artillery manufacture.
- **Navy and shipbuilding:** Ukraine has Black Sea yards and major shipbuilding. Georgia has Black Sea ports and coastal infrastructure. Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan have Caspian access, which supports flotilla, patrol, merchant and port options only. Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania have Baltic ports and maritime services; port access alone does not prove major warship construction. Landlocked Belarus, Moldova, Armenia, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan should have no naval production branch.
- **Electronics and research:** Armenia has computer, semiconductor and military electronics anchors; Ukraine has defense electronics; Latvia has VEF communications and consumer electronics; Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania developed PC projects mostly from 1977. Keep computing, radio and research separate from a full semiconductor supply chain unless the alternate path pays to build it.
- **Resources and heavy industry:** prioritize Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, Turkmenistan, Estonia, Belarus, Georgia, Armenia and Tajikistan by actual resource or processing base. Extraction is not equivalent to refining or export capacity. Represent transport, processing, water, imported inputs and Union market access as prerequisites or liabilities.
- **Civilian industry and agriculture:** all republics can modernize or diversify, but should receive country-specific levers such as Belarusian tractors and potash, Moldovan wine and fruit processing, Baltic electrical and food industries, Central Asian irrigation and cotton, Ukrainian grain and metallurgy, and Caucasus crops and mineral processing.

For an independence outcome, make the shock reduce **network utilization**, not delete the physical factories: legacy suppliers, design bureaus, technical standards, state orders, rail and fuel allocation, payment clearing, and customers can disappear or become foreign and costly. Sources on Ukrainian military industry describe incomplete production cycles and reliance on Russian microelectronics, rocket fuel and special alloys. Central Asian irrigation coupled upstream water with downstream fuel and electricity transfers. Tajik aluminum is an especially clear imported-input and electricity case. These are supply-chain and coordination penalties, not a claim that every plant instantly stops operating.

## Two-year shock-therapy option

**Historical fact:** Poland's rapid price liberalization and stabilization reduced very high inflation and shortages, but measured GDP fell about 12 percent in 1990 and industrial output fell sharply. The decline had several causes: demand compression, market prices exposing unsaleable or low-quality production, collapse of planning and CMEA/Soviet trade, and delayed new private supply. Private-sector recovery appeared later, not as a guaranteed immediate two-year productivity dividend. IMF retrospectives note that reformers overestimated how quickly output would respond to new incentives. [IMF, Poland transition overview](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781557756282/C01.xml); [IMF, structural and macroeconomic determinants, Poland 1990 to 1991](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/1992/086/article-A001-en.xml); [IMF, initial stabilization and reform](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/display/book/9781498305631/ch02.xml).

**Fiscal fact:** across former Soviet countries, central-government revenues fell substantially as output contracted, tax systems lost old collection channels, and Union financing and transfers ended. Privatization proceeds were usually too small to compensate for that revenue loss. A rapid sale could bring new management and working capital, but weak accounting, legal and tax institutions, insider acquisition, limited capital and absent buyers could instead transfer assets without investment. [IMF, Fiscal Transition in Countries of the Former Soviet Union](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/001/1996/061/article-A001-en.xml); [American Economic Association review of privatization evidence](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.47.3.699).

**Historically grounded policy choices:**

1. **Rapid liberalization and stabilization:** prices and exchange become meaningful quickly, shortages and wasteful production can fall, and private entry is easier. Near-term risks are inflation spikes, falling real wages, layoffs, demand collapse, lost tax revenue and unviable firms closing before new suppliers arise.
2. **Gradual prices with state enterprise support:** protects jobs, local supplier chains and tax base temporarily. It also risks preserving obsolete output, arrears, soft budget constraints, shortages and delayed productivity change. IMF accounts note that gradualists still experienced contraction and often lagged the recovery.
3. **Selective privatization with institution-building:** sell or lease viable small and medium enterprises early, while auditing large strategic firms, settling property claims, building tax administration and preserving competitively viable supply links. This is slower and politically contested, but avoids assuming that a sale itself creates productivity. Strategic sectors can choose foreign investment, domestic ownership, worker/manager ownership, or continued state control, each with capital, control and employment tradeoffs.

**Balance proposal:** a two-year “shock therapy productivity growth” reward is plausible only as a conditional long-run efficiency effect. It should be paired with immediate fiscal-capacity loss and social costs, and it should depend on stabilization credibility, working tax collection, trade access, legal enforcement, and whether firms have viable customers and input suppliers. Treat the two-year growth bonus as a game abstraction, not an empirical claim that Poland or the former Soviet republics grew in the first two years. Do not couple it automatically to mass privatization.

## Short historical references for universal specialization prose

These are optional final sentences for the nine universal programme descriptions. They name real examples and dates; examples establish historical precedents, not exclusive country eligibility or claims that each republic adopted the same policy.

- **Aircraft:** Ukraine's Antonov centers, Tbilisi's Su-25 production line from 1978, and Tashkent's serial Il-76 output from 1973 document distinct aircraft roles: [SIPRI Yearbook 1997](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/SIPRI%20Yearbook%201997.pdf); [Jane's, Tbilisi Aircraft Manufacturing](https://janes.migavia.com/ge/tam.html); [Uzbek Airways, Il-76 history](https://www.uzairways.com/en/print/pdf/node/2082).
- **Armour:** Kharkiv's serial T-64 production began in October 1963 and entered Soviet service in 1966: [ArmyInform, T-64 history](https://armyinform.com.ua/2020/09/11/t-64-ponad-50-rokiv-u-stroyu/); [Kharkiv Polytechnic, tank-building history](https://repository.kpi.kharkov.ua/items/7987dc01-011c-4842-a737-787ce356fecd).
- **Artillery:** Perm, in the RSFSR, remained an important artillery center; Motovilikha developed and made the Uragan launcher in the 1970s: [RAND, Motovilikhinskie Zavody appendix](https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RR3000/RR3099/RAND_RR3099z1.appendixes.pdf).
- **Army and logistics:** Tashkent command courses taught tactics, topography, engineering and automotive subjects; armored-vehicle maintenance entered the curriculum in 1965 to 1967: [Tashkent Higher Military Command School, 1918 to 1941](https://akad.uz/?lang=en&page_id=10155); [1945 to 1991](https://akad.uz/?lang=en&page_id=10182).
- **Navy:** the 1972 yard survey lists Mykolaiv shipyards producing helicopter carriers and destroyers, but Klaipeda as merchant-only: [Polmar, Soviet Shipbuilding and Shipyards](https://www.usni.org/magazines/proceedings/1972/may/soviet-shipbuilding-and-shipyards).
- **Agriculture:** 1970 Union statistics show Kazakhstan's 22.2 million tonne grain harvest at 9.8 centners per hectare and Moldova's 2.4 million tonnes at 29.3: [Soviet encyclopedia, 1970 grain production by republic](https://www.booksite.ru/fulltext/1/001/008/046/763.htm).
- **Civilian industry:** Minsk Tractor Works began MTZ-2 wheeled-tractor output in 1953; Chisinau's T-50B vineyard tractor entered serial assembly in 1962: [MTZ plant history](https://www.mtzequipment.com/about/plant/history); [Chisinau Tractor Plant history](https://tracom.md/en/pi-tracom/history/).
- **Resources:** Azerbaijan's Baku fields were a major oil center; Turkmenistan's Odzhak field connected to the Bukhara-Urals pipeline in 1966; Kazakhstan produced coal and metals: [Azerbaijani Ministry of Energy, oil industry history](https://minenergy.gov.az/en/neft/neft-senayesinin-inkisaf-tarixi); [Turkmen gas industry history](https://s3.amazonaws.com/rgi-documents/6dcc33786a9e8ef3a9901324b0b51e2a15babc0e.pdf); [Soviet encyclopedia, Kazakh SSR industry](https://www.booksite.ru/fulltext/1/001/008/057/580.htm).
- **Electronics:** Yerevan's Nairi computer entered production in 1964; Vilnius's Rūta computers date to 1964; Latvia's VEF made communications equipment, including Soviet-era telephone exchanges: [Mergelyan, Nairi computer family](https://academic.oup.com/itnow/article-abstract/15/1/14/8154792); [Lithuanian encyclopedia, Sigma](https://www.vle.lt/straipsnis/sigma/); [Kanger, Domestic PC Production in the Soviet Baltic States](https://arvutimuuseum.ee/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Domestic-PC-Production-in-the-Soviet-Baltic-States-1977-1992.pdf).
