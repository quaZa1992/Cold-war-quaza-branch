IMPERIA COLD WAR

# Greece in January 1970: historical research record

This record separates the January 1970 starting situation from later events. It supplies historical grounding for a concept review only. It does not prescribe effects or code. The 1974 Cyprus coup, later party formations, and Greece's later institutional departures are not treated as inevitable from the 1970 starting point.

## State and leadership in January 1970

Greece was a kingdom under military rule. Prime Minister Georgios Papadopoulos was the regime's central political figure and also held the Defence portfolio. The failed royal countercoup of 13 December 1967 forced King Constantine II into exile. Lieutenant General Georgios Zoitakis, appointed Regent on 13 December, continued to exercise royal functions in the King's absence. The 1968 constitutional text provided for the Regent to remain until parliamentary elections unless the government invited the King to return earlier; no election date had been set. Papadopoulos had not yet assumed the Regency; that occurred in March 1972. Treating him as Regent or President in January 1970 would be anachronistic. [The State Department's 13 December 1967 cable](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d344), [the following day's assessment](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d349), and [the 1968 constitution's Article 134 as reproduced in the Congressional Record](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1968-pt17/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1968-pt17-6-3.pdf) document the succession. The later U.S. diplomatic account dates Papadopoulos's assumption of Regency to March 1972. [FRUS, 22 March 1972](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d333).

The original junta leadership was not a unified civilian cabinet. Papadopoulos, Deputy Prime Minister Stylianos Pattakos, and economic coordinator Nikolaos Makarezos were the visible senior trio. The Revolutionary Council included other officers, and Papadopoulos's authority over them was real but contested. U.S. reporting describes an internal confrontation in September 1970 and reports that Papadopoulos emerged stronger after considering resignation. It is evidence of rivalry, not proof of a stable opposition party or a settled succession plan. [FRUS, 7 June 1971, retrospective on the regime's structure and 1970 divisions](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d314).

Dimitrios Ioannidis was already an influential security officer and head of the Greek Military Police, ESA. A later U.S. intelligence assessment says he had acted as a watchdog on Papadopoulos since at least 1970. That does not make him head of state or the undisputed leader of a separate junta faction at the January 1970 start. His decisive seizure of power was in November 1973. The distinction matters especially for Cyprus: the July 1974 coup was ordered under the later Ioannidis regime, after Papadopoulos had been removed. [FRUS assessment of Ioannidis and the 1973 change](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d9), [FRUS chronology of the July 1974 coup](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d148).

## Constitutional claims, coercion, and institutions

The junta promulgated a constitution submitted to a referendum on 29 September 1968. Its existence should not be confused with constitutional government. By 1970, parliamentary elections had not occurred, the political-party and parliamentary provisions were not operative, martial law remained in force, and emergency military courts retained jurisdiction over state-security cases. In a 9 April 1970 letter to President Nixon, Papadopoulos presented a timetable of gradual normalization: he said press censorship had been abolished in December 1969, assembly and association articles were being implemented, Article 10 on detention would enter into force that April, and provisions on military courts were expected later in 1970. This is the regime's own account, not independent confirmation that political rights were secure. [The official Greek catalog identifies the referendum text and date](https://opac.media.gov.gr/bib/7298); [Papadopoulos's 9 April 1970 letter](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d274); [a 1970 U.S. assessment identifies martial law as the remaining unfulfilled item in the timetable](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d286).

The reported liberalization was bounded. Preventive press censorship had formally been lifted in late 1969, but new press rules, criminal penalties, martial law, and uncertainty about what could be published continued to constrain journalists. Political parties and elections were absent. Political detainees remained, and U.S. reporting in 1971 records that Papadopoulos still rejected lifting martial law for state-security offenses. This 1971 evidence should be cited as subsequent confirmation, not treated as information available to a January 1970 actor. [Contemporary U.S. reporting on press controls in November 1969](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1969-pt26/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1969-pt26-1-3.pdf), [FRUS on the martial-law position in 1971](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d302). The Council of Europe case against Greece concerned Convention violations, including torture; Greece notified the Council of its withdrawal on 12 December 1969, but membership ended only on 31 December 1970. [Council of Europe historical summary](https://70.coe.int/1969-greece-withdraws-en.html), [official Resolution 70 (34)](https://rm.coe.int/16804bc2a7).

The Orthodox Church was both a central Greek institution and a contested site of junta control. The dictatorship intervened in Church governance and pushed out Archbishop Chrysostomos II, installing Ieronymos, a figure it regarded as more cooperative. The episode shows that the regime's Orthodox and "Hellenic-Christian" claims should not be read as evidence of autonomous Church endorsement. The available source is a later scholarly dissertation in the Greek National Archive of PhD Theses, whose English abstract describes the intervention and succession. [EKT thesis record and English abstract](https://phdtheses.ekt.gr/eadd/handle/10442/39083).

## Party traditions and opposition networks

No ordinary national parties could campaign legally in January 1970. Party histories are still useful as social and political traditions, but they should not be presented as freely operating parliamentary organizations in the starting situation.

The last pre-coup parliamentary election was held on 16 February 1964. The centrist Centre Union (EK), led by Georgios Papandreou, won 52.72 percent and 174 seats; the conservative National Radical Union (ERE), then led by Panagiotis Kanellopoulos, won 35.26 percent and 102 seats; and the United Democratic Left (EDA), associated with the communist movement, won 11.80 percent and 22 seats. The Centre Union had been a broad coalition with internal divisions, not a tightly unified ideological party. The 1965 defections and political crisis preceded the April 1967 coup. [ERT Archive's 1964 election record](https://www.ert.gr/ert-arxeio/voyleytikes-ekloges-16-fevroyarioy-1964/), [FRUS overview of ERE and Centre Union traditions](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d28).

The Communist Party of Greece (KKE) had been illegal since the Civil War era. EDA served as an electoral and public vehicle for communist influence before the junta dissolved it. In February 1968, the clandestine KKE split: an exile leadership retained the KKE name and Moscow-aligned orientation, while dissident cadres established the KKE Interior. These were distinct underground and exile networks, not legal electoral parties in 1970. The split's interpretation is politically contested; the KKE's own history calls the departing group revisionist, while academic accounts describe a dispute over organization, domestic strategy, and the 1968 Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia. [KKE history of the 1968 split](https://inter.kke.gr/en/m-article/65340078-bb2b-11f0-b1e1-be037ce642f9/), [peer-reviewed account of the Greek radical left split](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/57873/1/57873.pdf).

Andreas Papandreou was arrested after the coup, released, and left Greece in January 1968. He founded the Panhellenic Liberation Movement (PAK) abroad as an anti-dictatorship organization. PAK was not PASOK: Papandreou founded PASOK only on 3 September 1974 after the regime fell. Likewise, the conservative New Democracy party did not exist in 1970; Konstantinos Karamanlis founded it on 4 October 1974. A pre-1970 design can refer to Centre Union or ERE constituencies and leaders, or to PAK as an exile resistance network, while clearly marking any later party identity as a post-dictatorship development. [Greek Prime Minister's official biography of Andreas Papandreou](https://www.primeminister.gr/prime-minister/andreas-papandreou), [PASOK's founding chronology](https://pasok.gr/history/), [New Democracy's founding chronology](https://nd.gr/politikos-organismos/istoria/).

## Economic starting conditions

Greece entered 1970 amid strong growth, not a collapse. A World Bank assessment dated 31 March 1970 estimated GNP growth above 8 percent for that year and described the recovery after the 1967-68 slowdown. Later OECD data put 1970 GDP shares at about 20 percent agriculture, 28 percent industry, and 52 percent services. Agriculture remained a major employer even as manufacturing and construction expanded. Shipping, tourism, emigrant remittances, and foreign capital supported foreign exchange and investment, while rural underemployment, fragmented small farms, and weak international competitiveness remained structural concerns. [World Bank, Greece: Current Economic Position and Prospects, March 1970](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/343671468250304568), [OECD Economic Survey: Greece 1972, basic statistics for 1970](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/1972/11/oecd-economic-surveys-greece-1972_g1g16e97/eco_surveys-grc-1972-en.pdf), [World Bank discussion of structural and sectoral challenges](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/172171468030633627/pdf/multi0page.pdf). The OECD's 1972 survey and the World Bank's 1975 report include retrospective measurements, so their exact statistics are useful for the period but were not all knowable in January 1970.

The economy therefore supports a historically grounded tension between growth and uneven modernization. A portrayal of immediate economic ruin, universal unemployment, or a fully industrial economy would miss the contemporary evidence. So would treating aggregate growth as proof that the regime enjoyed popular consent: elections and opinion polling were not available to measure that. [FRUS's 1971 intelligence estimate explicitly notes the absence of free elections and opinion polls](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d314).

## Foreign institutions: three separate timelines

**European Economic Community.** Greece's Association Agreement was signed on 9 July 1961 and entered into force on 1 November 1962. After the 1967 coup, the Community restricted further political and economic integration and withheld new financial assistance, but specific treaty obligations, including scheduled tariff treatment, continued. In January 1970 Greece was not an EEC member, nor was the association completely terminated. The phrase "frozen association" is convenient but can overstate the suspension if it implies that every trade provision stopped. [EUR-Lex treaty record](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=oj%3AJOP_1963_026_0294_002), [European Parliament Research Service history of the limited suspension](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/BRIE/2021/679064/EPRS_BRI%282021%29679064_EN.pdf).

**Council of Europe.** This was a human-rights institution, separate from the EEC. Greece announced withdrawal on 12 December 1969 after pressure over the Convention case. The withdrawal legally took effect at the end of 1970. Thus Greece remained a member at the start of January 1970 during the notice period, but had declared its exit. [Council of Europe Resolution 70 (34)](https://rm.coe.int/16804bc2a7).

**NATO.** Greece remained a NATO member and continued to meet its alliance commitments in January 1970. The later 1974 action was a withdrawal of Greek forces from NATO's integrated military command structure, not withdrawal from the North Atlantic Treaty. Greece returned to the integrated structure in 1980. Do not backdate the 1974 decision or make it a January 1970 status. [NATO history of Greece and the Alliance](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/nato-history/history-by-theme/my-country-and-nato/greece-and-nato), [FRUS record of the 1974 command withdrawal](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v21/d199).

## Cyprus: a real policy issue, not a predetermined coup

Cyprus was an independent republic with its own president and communal constitutional framework, not Greek territory. Greece was one of the guarantor powers under the 1960 settlement. Greek officers served in Cyprus's National Guard, creating a channel of influence and a potential conflict of command. In March 1970, the U.S. Embassy in Nicosia reported evidence that some Greek officers on Cyprus and some mainland officers, including Aslanides, were plotting a "double Enosis" course and attempting to create trouble for the leadership in Athens. The same dispatch cautioned that it had not found indications that the highest levels of the Greek government had adopted that plan. This is evidence for factional or officer-led pressure, not proof of a settled 1970 policy or an agreed Greek-Turkish plan. [FRUS, Embassy Nicosia telegram, 17 March 1970](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d352).

For Greek nationalist officers, Enosis could be framed as a strategic and national objective. But the 1970 record also shows why the issue carried serious risks: undermining an independent neighboring state, bypassing Greek Cypriot and Turkish Cypriot political agency, provoking Turkey, and dividing Athens's own regime. At that date EOKA-B did not yet exist. Grivas returned clandestinely to Cyprus in 1971 and established the organization later. It is a major anachronism to treat EOKA-B as an active January 1970 actor. [Library of Congress country study on EOKA-B's formation in 1971](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/frd/frdcstdy/cy/cypruscountrystu00sols/cypruscountrystu00sols.pdf), [Makarios's 2 July 1974 letter, later published in an archival collection](https://mfa.gov.ct.tr/wp-content/uploads/books/human_rights_in_cyprus.pdf).

The actual 15 July 1974 coup was organized under Ioannidis's regime, which had taken power in November 1973. The 1974 U.S. diplomatic record shows Ioannidis claimed he chose to assist Greek Cypriot nationalists after their request and amid his view that Makarios might expel Greek officers; the same record documents his attempt to disclaim prior planning, so his account should be treated as a self-justifying statement. The coup was followed by Turkey's 20 July operation, which Ankara said it undertook under the Treaty of Guarantee. The U.N. Security Council called for a ceasefire, an end to foreign military intervention, and talks among the guarantor states. Those events are essential later consequences to know, but they do not establish that a lawful or automatic war response followed from the treaty. [FRUS, U.S. envoy's meeting with Ioannidis, 16 July 1974](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d88), [UNFICYP chronology of the coup, Turkish operation, and Security Council response](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/events-summer-1974).

The chronology should therefore preserve at least three distinct stages: real but uncertain officer-level Cyprus plotting documented in 1970; formation of EOKA-B and later escalation after Grivas's 1971 return; and the separate 1974 coup decision by Ioannidis's post-1973 regime. None of these makes the next step inevitable.

## Source and interpretation cautions

- U.S. diplomatic and intelligence records are valuable contemporary evidence but reflect U.S. priorities, imperfect information, and occasional reliance on sources that the reporting officers themselves describe as uncertain. The March 1970 Cyprus cable expressly distinguishes officer activity from approval at the highest Greek levels.
- Greek government statements about constitutional normalization and economic performance should be attributed to the government. They do not independently establish free political conditions.
- Party histories maintained by the later parties are useful for dates and organizational claims but may frame ideological disputes in partisan terms. The KKE account of the 1968 split is an example.
- Later economic tables can describe 1970 outcomes, but not what policymakers could know at the opening date. Keep measured results separate from contemporary estimates.
- Later outcomes such as Ioannidis's seizure of power, EOKA-B's operations, the 1974 coup, the Turkish intervention, and the NATO command withdrawal should be dated explicitly whenever mentioned.
