# Enosis, partition proposals, and the 1960 settlement

Short historical note for evaluating alternative Greek-Junta/Cyprus political paths. This is historical context, not legal advice or a claim that any path was automatically lawful.

## Treaty framework and British bases

The 16 August 1960 Treaty of Guarantee committed the Republic of Cyprus to preserve its independence, territorial integrity, security, and Constitution. Article I prohibited participation in any political or economic union with any state and prohibited activity promoting either union with another state or partition of the island. Article II had Greece, Turkey, and the United Kingdom recognize and guarantee Cyprus's independence and territorial integrity. The Treaty of Guarantee is distinct from the 1960 Treaty of Alliance and from NATO. [UN Treaty Series, Treaty concerning the Establishment and Treaty of Guarantee](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20382/v382.pdf).

The 1960 Treaty of Establishment transferred sovereignty over the Republic, but excluded the Akrotiri and Dhekelia Sovereign Base Areas from Cypriot territory and retained them under British sovereignty. Neither full Enosis nor a partitioned-south arrangement would automatically transfer those areas to Greece or Cyprus; a change would require a separate British agreement. [Treaty of Establishment, Articles 1-2](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20382/v382.pdf); [UK Cyprus Act 1960, section 2](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Eliz2/8-9/52/contents).

## 1964: Enosis proposals and their terms

Dean Acheson's July 1964 mediation ideas explicitly explored union of Cyprus with Greece coupled with Turkish security and territorial concessions, including a possible Turkish sovereign base in the Karpas Peninsula and protections or autonomy for Turkish Cypriots. Acheson presented these as proposals to Greek and Turkish representatives, not an agreed settlement. Greece rejected a Turkish sovereign area as unacceptable and favored a leased base; the August revision also failed to produce agreement. Makarios was not a party to the Greek-Turkish Geneva negotiations and opposed the proposal as a modified form of partition. This is a real precedent for an Enosis-for-concessions bargain, but not evidence that the Turkish Cypriot community had consented or that union was legally automatic. [FRUS, Acheson to the Department of State, 14 July 1964](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d85); [FRUS, Acheson's report on Greek objections, 6 August 1964](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d103); [Library of Congress, Cyprus country study](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcmassbookdig.cypruscountrystu00sols/?q=cyprus&sp=72&st=text).

“Double Enosis” meant partitioning Cyprus between Greece and Turkey, rather than simply joining a Greek-controlled southern zone to Greece. U.S. officials discussed that as a possible strategic bargain during the 1964 crisis. On 19 July 1974, Secretary of State Kissinger told a colleague that if Turkey obtained a portion of the island, he thought the United States would have to work for “double enosis” and give Greece the other part. That was his proposal in an internal conversation, not a treaty, accepted plan, or settled U.S. policy. [FRUS, 19 July 1974 telephone conversation](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d100).

## Makarios and Sampson in July 1974

Makarios's position should be described with care. In May 1974 he reportedly said that, if freely choosing between Enosis and independence, he would choose Enosis, calling independence a compromise. But in his 2 July letter to Greek President Gizikis he protested Greek officers' support for EOKA-B and demanded their withdrawal; after the 15 July coup, he told the UN Security Council that the Greek junta had violated Cyprus's independence and extended its dictatorship to the island. In that immediate crisis he defended the Republic and its sovereignty against a coup that he identified with Athens, not a consensual constitutional union. [Contemporary letter reproduced in the Congressional Record](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1974-pt18/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1974-pt18-4-2.pdf); [Makarios's Security Council address, S/PV.1780](https://documents.un.org/api/symbol/access?l=en&s=S%2FPV.1780%28OR%29&t=pdf); [contemporary report quoting the May interview](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200405/cmselect/cmfaff/113/113we23.htm).

Nikos Sampson was installed as acting President after the 15 July coup and was associated with the Enosis movement. The coup authorities initially represented their government as an internal Cyprus change, promised to continue intercommunal talks, and denied that immediate Enosis was intended, while proclaiming Greek Cypriot “national unity.” U.S. reporting records that this public line was designed to reassure Turkey and other governments; Ankara nevertheless regarded the coup as a major step toward Enosis. A later report to the UN cites Sampson as saying in 1975 that he had been about to proclaim Enosis before resigning; treat that as retrospective evidence, not as an official July 1974 proclamation. [FRUS, 15 July 1974 assessment](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d79); [FRUS, 15 July 1974 Ankara assessment](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d85); [UN document citing the later Sampson statement](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/437008/files/A_55_866--S_2001_307-EN.pdf).

## Historical limits of the two proposed alternatives

- **Full Cyprus-Greece union:** Enosis was a longstanding nationalist objective and the 1964 Acheson proposals show that outside actors explored it in exchange for Turkish bases, security guarantees, or community arrangements. The 1974 coup made the threat concrete to Turkey and was followed by Turkish military action. It remains an alternate-history outcome: the 1960 settlement prohibited union, and no lawful automatic accession or guaranteed Turkish response followed merely from a Greek focus or coup.
- **Union of a Greek-controlled south after de facto partition:** This resembles “double Enosis” only at a broad territorial level. No recognized 1974 settlement divided Cyprus into a Greek-union south and a Turkish-union north. After the coup and Turkey's military operations of 20 July and August 1974, the Republic remained internationally recognized as the state for Cyprus; the North's separate declaration came only in 1983, and Security Council Resolution 541 deemed it legally invalid and called for its withdrawal. The declaration was recognized by Turkey alone. A southern-only union is therefore an additional alternate-history partition and annexation bargain, not a historical fact or an agreed outcome. It would also implicate Turkish Cypriot political consent and British sovereignty over the SBAs. [UNFICYP chronology of summer 1974](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/node/73113); [Security Council Resolution 541 (1983)](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/58970?v=pdf).

For both options, the historical sources support nationalist incentives, negotiation precedents, and severe escalation risk. They do not establish automatic legality, Turkish consent, Turkish Cypriot consent, or British consent.
