# Greek personnel and arms in Cyprus: historical record

This is a short historical source note for evaluating a proposed Greek transfer of personnel and equipment to the Republic of Cyprus before any Turkish landing. It does not describe a game mechanic or establish a legal conclusion.

## Treaty baseline: ELDYK and the alliance contingent

The [1960 Treaty of Alliance](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20397/volume-397-I-5712-English.pdf), signed by Cyprus, Greece and Turkey on 16 August 1960, established a Tripartite Headquarters in Cyprus. Article IV set Greek and Turkish contingents at the numbers in Additional Protocol I: 950 Greek and 650 Turkish officers, non-commissioned officers and soldiers. The stated task of these contingents was training the Cypriot army. Protocol I says the Republic's President and Vice-President, acting in agreement, could request Greece and Turkey to increase or reduce their contingents. The Greek contingent was organized as the Greek Force in Cyprus, ELDYK; the Greek force's official history dates its formation to 1959 and its arrival to 16 August 1960. [Greek Ministry of Defence history of ELDYK](https://www.gov.cy/amyna/chairetismos-tou-ypourgou-amynas-k-michali-giorgalla-stin-ekdilosi-pros-timin-tis-eldyk-sta-lefkara-chthes/); [UNFICYP account of the 950/650 contingents](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/establishment-unficyp).

The Alliance Treaty is separate from the [Treaty of Guarantee](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20382/v382.pdf). The Guarantee Treaty does not set a force-delivery program. Its Article IV provides for consultation among guarantors following a breach and refers to action with the sole aim of re-establishing the treaty state of affairs. It should not be presented as automatically authorizing a Greek force expansion, military aid, or intervention. There is also a status caveat: after intercommunal fighting began, Cyprus declared the Alliance Treaty abrogated on 4 April 1964; Greece and Turkey nevertheless kept their contingents on the island. UNFICYP records this disputed episode and the continuing presence. [UNFICYP, Establishment of UNFICYP](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/establishment-unficyp).

## Additional Greek forces in 1964

The Cypriot National Guard was established by cabinet decree on 4 June 1964. The Cypriot National Guard's official history identifies the first commander as Greek Lt. Gen. Georgios Karayiannis and records Greek officers in its early command structure. [Republic of Cyprus National Guard history](https://www.gov.cy/army/documents/ethniki-froyra/60-chronia-ef/).

During summer 1964, Greece sent mainland personnel beyond the Treaty contingent. A contemporaneous U.S. National Security Council memorandum on 7 July estimated 4,000 additional Greek troops on Cyprus, with CIA estimates of 5,000 to 7,000, besides the regular contingent; it also said Greek officers commanded the Cypriot National Guard. By 3 September, a U.S. State Department telegram described around 10,000 Greek troops on the island, distinct from a 30,000-strong Greek Cypriot National Guard. UN Secretary-General reporting in September separately estimated that about 5,000 Greek nationals had arrived during July and been included within the National Guard strength; among them were Greek Army officers, instructors and technicians. The reports count different categories and use uncertain estimates, so “a Greek division” is a convenient later shorthand, not a consistently documented exact order of battle. [FRUS, 7 July 1964](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d82); [FRUS, 3 September 1964](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d152); [UN Secretary-General report S/5950 (10 September 1964)](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n64/186/63/pdf/n6418663.pdf?OpenElement).

This is a precedent for Greek personnel and equipment being present beyond ELDYK, but it was a crisis-period force buildup, not the routine 950-person treaty mission. Reports described the new personnel as entering in excess of the treaty limits; the scale and exact composition are estimates. It should be represented as a deliberate, politically consequential alternative, not as an automatic entitlement under the Guarantee Treaty.

## Withdrawal after the 1967 crisis

After fighting at Agios Theodoros and Kophinou on 15 November 1967, Turkey threatened intervention and demanded withdrawal of Greek troops beyond the agreed contingent. The Secretary-General's appeals called for withdrawal of forces in excess of the national contingents. Greece and Turkey agreed to withdraw their excess forces; UNFICYP dates Greek troop repatriation from 8 December 1967 through 16 January 1968. Since the states conducted the withdrawal themselves and there was no agreement on reciprocity, UNFICYP did not independently verify that all excess troops had left. [UNFICYP chronology of the 1967 crisis](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/1967-crisis); [FRUS, U.S. Embassy report on the November 1967 force-reduction talks](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d314); [FRUS, December 1967 account of Greek generals opposing withdrawal](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d343).

## Historical boundary for a 1970 concept

The defensible historical baseline is ELDYK's treaty-sized contingent, with a separately documented but exceptional precedent for larger Greek personnel deployments in 1964–67. Two Cypriot light infantry units receiving Greek personnel and weapons as a pre-landing transfer would be a new, negotiated counterfactual arrangement. The sources support that Greek military involvement could take several forms, but do not show a routine treaty mechanism by which Greece could simply hand Cyprus two formed battalions. Keep the Republic's own consent and the distinction between its forces and Greek troops clear. Do not claim that the Treaty of Guarantee automatically made an enlarged deployment lawful.
