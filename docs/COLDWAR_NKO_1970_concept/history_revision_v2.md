# DPRK foreign-diplomacy concepts, 1970–1994: historical revision

This supplement supports concept work only. Historical claims below are separated from proposed game abstraction. The date window ends with Kim Il Sung’s death on 8 July 1994; the later famine and fully developed Songun framework must not be projected backward into the 1970 start.

## Record and design guardrails

The 1961 Soviet and Chinese treaties were unusually strong: they included consultation, economic/technical cooperation and an armed-assistance clause if the other party suffered armed attack and entered a state of war. The Chinese treaty remains in force unless both parties agree to amend or terminate it; the Soviet treaty was initially for ten years with automatic five-year extensions. The NKO diplomacy concepts here should not silently recreate those obligations: the requested gameplay packages are voluntary economic/scientific/military-technical agreements with no automatic war entry and no sanctions in either direction. Treat them as narrower, newly negotiated projects, not as proof that old treaties were absent. [UN Treaty Series, USSR–DPRK treaty](https://treaties.un.org/doc/Publication/UNTS/Volume%20420/volume-420-I-6045-English.pdf); [1961 PRC–DPRK treaty text](https://worldjpn.net/documents/texts/docs/19610711.T1E.html); [Korean History Database treaty records and context](https://contents.history.go.kr/front/hm/view.do?levelId=hm_152_0050).

For each proposed offer, make the foreign-country decision explicit and optional. Acceptance should apply one persistent spirit to each country, with distinct reciprocal effects and burdens; refusal should do nothing to relations or diplomacy. No third-party actor should be pulled into a war, alliance, embargo, or sanctions action by the event. This is a game abstraction: real agreements varied in duration and enforcement. A later treaty withdrawal or project cancellation can be represented by an explicit bilateral decision rather than a hidden timer.

## Historically grounded bilateral package candidates

All costs below are balance proposals, not historical budget figures. DPRK national accounts are uncertain, so express costs as a small share of the participating country’s GDP, not a fabricated dollar total. Suggested recurring GDP shares are annual economic commitments, converted to the mod’s weekly MD budget unit during implementation. Keep the GDP cost visibly attached to the country paying it. The recipient choice is the foreign state’s consent to a bounded cooperation package; the DPRK decision to tender it is separate.

### Japan and Chongryon: a distinct non-state channel

Do not create a Japan–DPRK state treaty or imply normalization. Japan’s 1971 Bluebook stated Japan had no diplomatic relations with the DPRK while noting expanding economic, cultural and sports contacts; its 1974 Bluebook reported bilateral two-way trade of about US$360 million despite no diplomatic relations. Japan’s 1980 Bluebook reports a private fisheries interim agreement, negotiated between a Japanese fisheries association and the North Korean side, as one example of practical contact without diplomatic recognition. [Japan MFA, 1971 Bluebook](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/bluebook/1971/s46-2-1-1.htm); [Japan MFA, 1974 Bluebook](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/other/bluebook/1974/1974-3-1.htm); [Japan MFA, 1980 Bluebook](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/bluebook/1980/s55-2010101-3.htm). Chongryon and affiliated private networks can be represented as a diaspora/trade channel with remittances, commercial intermediation and political links, but should not be presented as the Japanese government or receive a country-wide paired national spirit. An optional state decision could be a narrow commercial/fisheries protocol, not normalization; if the game lacks non-state actors, use a domestic NKO spirit without a paired Japan spirit.

## Domestic and foreign-policy context for route structure

The July 1972 statement arose from mutual détente, secret contacts and the wish of both governments to limit superpower leverage; it was followed by talks but broke down amid accusations and competing interpretations. A 1980 confederal proposal should be represented as an offer that can be refused, not a focus reward claiming unification. The 1984 Joint Venture Law created a legal opening for foreign investment, but the climate remained constrained by state control and poor confidence after hard-currency debt defaults in the mid-1970s. [Korean History Database, joint venture legislation materials](https://db.history.go.kr/diachronic/level.do?levelId=kn_027_0080_0030_0010); [IDE/JETRO, Success and Failure of North Korean Development Strategy](https://ir.ide.go.jp/record/51647/files/IDP000769_001.pdf).

DPRK joined the NPT on 12 December 1985, as civilian nuclear cooperation with the USSR was part of the inducement and context. The joining did not equal full transparency or solve the security dilemma. A late-1980s/early-1990s civilian-power cooperation spirit can therefore pair energy/technical gains with safeguards, inspection and political-confidence burdens; do not grant a nuclear weapon benefit from this choice. [IAEA, DPRK safeguards and NPT report](https://www.iaea.org/sites/default/files/dprk.pdf).

The late-1960s and 1970s defense burden was a central opportunity cost for industrial policy. Historical documents cite very high military-spending claims, though DPRK budget numbers are opaque and propaganda may distort them. A game spirit can express the trade-off as industrial investment versus readiness/fortification, not a precise historical defense percentage. [CWIHP Bulletin, DPRK/GDR records](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/publication/CWIHP_Bulletin_14-15.pdf).

## Counterfactual armed reunification route: guardrails

An offensive against the South is an alternate-history route, not the historical continuation of détente. It can be grounded in the DPRK leadership’s 1960s coercive strategy and 1968 infiltration/Blue House raid, the 1968 USS *Pueblo* seizure and the regime’s stated intention to “liberate” the South. But by 1970 Seoul’s military and economic position was changing, the United States maintained forces and treaty commitments, and neither Moscow nor Beijing should be presumed to endorse a renewed war merely because Pyongyang chooses it. [CWIHP Bulletin, East German reporting on DPRK policy and 1960s escalation](https://www.wilsoncenter.org/sites/default/files/media/documents/publication/CWIHP_Bulletin_14-15.pdf); [FRUS, 1969–76 Korea documents](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v19p1).

If represented, let the player choose escalation and expose the costs: war risk, trade interruption, economic mobilization, possible diplomatic isolation, and a low-probability/poorly controllable military outcome. Foreign countries should make their own responses through recipient/diplomatic decisions; the game must not force Soviet or Chinese intervention, force a foreign player to take sides, or fire sanctions as an automatic result. Preserve the peaceful route as a genuine opt-in alternative, with no punitive hidden check for declining war.

## Leadership succession and leader art reference

Kim Il Sung died on 8 July 1994. The U.S. Secretary’s Morning Intelligence Summary dated 9 July said Kim Jong Il had long directed most state affairs, was named “great successor” in the death announcement and was expected to continue policy, while noting no unusual military activity and predicting continuity. In July 1994, Kim Jong Il should therefore be described as de facto leader/successor, not as already holding every formal title. He had been designated heir in 1974 and publicly formalized as successor at the 1980 Sixth Party Congress; he became KPA Supreme Commander in 1991, NDC chairman in 1993, and WPK General Secretary only on 8 October 1997. [U.S. National Security Archive, Secretary’s Morning Intelligence Summary, 9 July 1994](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB421/docs/19940709.pdf); [Korean Ministry of Unification North Korea Information Portal](https://nkinfo.unikorea.go.kr/nkp/pge/view.do?menuId=PO005); [KBS timeline of Kim Jong Il’s posts](https://world.kbs.co.kr/special/northkorea/contents/archives/supreme_leader/kim_jong_il.htm); [Washington Post, 8 October 1997 formal appointment](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1997/10/09/north-koreas-kim-is-formally-named-to-leadership-post/2b277a99-03b0-406c-b71b-c01ca47d1bab/).
