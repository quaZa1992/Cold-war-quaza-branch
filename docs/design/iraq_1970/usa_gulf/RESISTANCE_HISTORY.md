# Kurdish resistance without a sovereign Kurdish state

Historical evidence and design boundaries. Researched 18 October 2026. This note does not implement or validate game mechanics.

## Historical findings

**An organized insurgency did not require a recognized Kurdish country.** A contemporary CIA background paper describes Barzani's guerrilla movement, its political organization, Iranian supply links, repeated fighting with Baghdad in the 1960s, and the incomplete settlement of March 1970. It also distinguishes a movement's armed regional autonomy from sovereign statehood. This supports representing Kurdish organization inside Iraqi-controlled territory before any separate country is released. It does not establish that every Kurdish civilian supported every faction, or that resistance was uniformly extreme throughout the north. [CIA background paper, 30 May 1972, FRUS document 310](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve04/d310)

**US financial and material support in 1972–75 is documented fact.** Kissinger's memorandum to Nixon of 24 June 1974 confirms that assistance began in July 1972, continued alongside substantially greater Iranian aid, and helped the movement resist Baghdad. Nixon approved a further FY1975 allocation. The historical beneficiaries were an existing armed movement, not a newly recognized state. [Kissinger to Nixon, FRUS document 254](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d254)

**Foreign support was vulnerable to its sponsors' diplomacy.** Algiers in March 1975 exchanged Iran's acceptance of ending support for Iraqi Kurdish forces for Iraq's acceptance of the thalweg boundary in the Shatt al-Arab. A renewed sponsorship mechanic should therefore allow interrupted supply, withdrawal of commitments, and bargaining with Baghdad rather than unlimited permanent external aid. [Contemporary INR assessment, April 1976](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v27/d306)

**Resistance later revived, but 1987–88 was not an uncomplicated US-sponsored episode.** Human Rights Watch's original investigation, drawing on captured Iraqi documents and survivor interviews, records renewed Kurdish organization during the Iran-Iraq War, KDP and PUK relations with Iran, and substantial rural support outside Baghdad's principal population centers and roads. It also documents the destruction, displacement, and genocide of the Anfal period. These events cannot be represented honestly as harmless administrative efficiency or as a permanent beneficial security reform. [HRW, original 1993 investigation](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/iraqanfal/ANFALINT.htm)

**The 1991 uprising was locally initiated after Iraqi military defeat.** US Army history describes civilian demonstrations, local defections, and subsequent Peshmerga participation; the coalition initially offered no military assistance to the revolt. Bush's 15 February appeal encouraged Iraqis and their military to remove Saddam, but did not constitute an explicit guarantee to arm Kurdish rebels. Bush still rejected military intervention in the civil war on 3 April. [US Army CMH, printed pages 29–30](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D101-PURL-gpo5268/pdf/GOVPUB-D101-PURL-gpo5268.pdf), [Bush, 15 February](https://www.bush41library.gov/digital-research-room/finding-aid/public-papers/remarks-raytheon-missile-systems-plant-employees), [Bush, 3 April](https://www.bush41library.gov/digital-research-room/finding-aid/public-papers/question-and-answer-session-reporters-hobe-sound)

**Protection followed repression and refugee flight.** In April, US and coalition humanitarian action expanded into protected camps and flight restrictions. This helped create conditions for later self-administration; Kurdish parties organized elections on 19 May 1992. Resolution 688 demanded an end to repression and humanitarian access; it did not expressly authorize a no-fly zone, autonomy, or independence. [Bush, 16 April](https://www.bush41library.gov/digital-research-room/finding-aid/public-papers/remarks-assistance-iraqi-refugees-and-news), [Kurdistan Parliament institutional history](https://www.parliament.krd/english/about-parliament/history-of-parliament/), [UN Resolution 688](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/110659?ln=en)

## Historically grounded gameplay interpretation

- An external sponsor can fund an existing clandestine political and armed organization while Kurdistan is absent as a separate game country. Local organization, unresolved grievances, supply access, and Iraqi military commitments should determine the resulting intensity; ethnicity alone should not.
- Very high resistance is a possible escalated condition, not a uniform January 1970 starting fact or an automatic consequence of one US focus. Funding should improve movement capacity while consuming actual sponsor funds and depending on continuing routes and commitments.
- Costly Iraqi responses can include accountable local policing, supply-route security, protection and repair of civilian infrastructure, paid local administration, enforceable autonomy negotiations, or limited operations against armed formations. Each trades treasury resources, equipment, political capital, military availability, or central control for specific results. These are design proposals, not measurements taken from the sources.
- A mass uprising should require local mobilization and a major state-control crisis, such as military defeat or elite fragmentation. Foreign encouragement can increase the opportunity; it should not guarantee a scripted rebellion or victory.
- For 1991, distinguish historical encouragement and later refugee protection from an optional stronger US decision to finance or arm a revolt. The latter is an alternative intervention, not a verified account of the initial historical uprising.
- Repression, civilian displacement, and destroyed livelihoods require serious consequences. Do not turn collective punishment or atrocities into reward mechanics.
