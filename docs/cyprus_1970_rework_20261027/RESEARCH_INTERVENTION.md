IMPERIA COLD WAR — Turkey, Greece and NATO intervention research

# Turkey, Greece and NATO intervention: historical boundaries

This research supports a proposed Cyprus crisis extension. It separates documented precedent from player-directed alternate history and numeric balance effects. It is not a legal opinion.

## What the record supports

- **5 June 1964: Turkey considered intervention, then postponed its decision.** President Lyndon Johnson's message to Prime Minister İsmet İnönü records that Turkey was contemplating military action to occupy part of Cyprus. Johnson urged consultation and warned that an intervention could produce a Greek-Turkish clash and possible Soviet involvement. The American ambassador's contemporaneous report says İnönü initially called his decision firm, then agreed to delay the plan for 24 hours so Washington could respond. The threatened intervention did not occur then. This is a sound precedent for Turkish operational planning, diplomatic pressure and an outside-power deterrent, not for a Turkish landing in 1964 or NATO expulsion. [Johnson message, FRUS, 5 June 1964](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d54); [Embassy report on İnönü's position and postponement, FRUS, 5 June 1964](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d53).
- **15–16 November 1967: a new escalation warning followed the Kophinou/Ayios Theodoros fighting.** The US State Department's retrospective editorial note records fighting on 15 November, a Turkish demand the next day that attacks stop, and a threat of military intervention. The US then sent Cyrus Vance to mediate among Ankara, Athens and Nicosia. This supports a crisis diplomacy and readiness choice, not an actual Turkish invasion that year. [FRUS, document 307](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v16/d307); [UNFICYP historical overview](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/1967-crisis).
- **10–12 March 1971: Turkey's military intervened in Turkish domestic politics.** A contemporaneous US intelligence cable describes a senior-command meeting, competing proposals for direct takeover or an ultimatum-backed government under nominal civilian rule, and officers who favored immediate intervention. The Demirel government resigned after the 12 March military memorandum. The record concerns Turkish domestic political unrest, not a Cyprus decision. It shows a historical basis for portraying military pressure as a possible institutional factor in Turkey; it does not establish a Cyprus-specific “Angry Army” reaction to restraint in 1974. [FRUS, Intelligence Information Cable, 10 March 1971](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v29/d441).
- **15–20 July 1974: the Cyprus coup and the Turkish intervention were distinct decisions.** UNFICYP's chronology dates the Greek-officer-directed National Guard coup against President Makarios to 15 July. On 20 July, US envoy Joseph Sisco reported that Turkey had already decided to intervene. Prime Minister Bülent Ecevit said he would consult his Council of Ministers; after that meeting, he told Sisco that the decision was irrevocable. Sisco also reported that Ecevit would consult the military and Cabinet about a ceasefire. This supports a Turkish government choice, preceded by readiness and diplomacy. It does not show an automatic intervention, a Turkish military coup threat over nonintervention, or that Turkey's claim under the Guarantee Treaty was uncontested. [UNFICYP 1974 chronology](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/events-summer-1974); [Sisco's contemporaneous 20 July account, FRUS document 104](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d104); [US policy before the landing, FRUS document 97](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d97).

## NATO: distinguish alliance membership from command integration

Greece's 1974 protest was a withdrawal from NATO's **integrated military structure**, not from the Alliance. A 16 September 1974 US cable reports that Greek forces were withdrawn from assignment or commitment to NATO effective 14 August, while Greek representatives remained in the Military Committee. NATO's history likewise says Greece remained a member and returned to the integrated structure in 1980. The official NATO account links the Greek decision to public resentment at NATO's response to Cyprus. Turkey did not leave NATO in this period. [NATO history of Greece and NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/nato-history/history-by-theme/my-country-and-nato/greece-and-nato); [FRUS cable on the Greek withdrawal, 16 September 1974](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d25).

The proposed mechanic in which **both Greece and Turkey leave full NATO membership temporarily during a war and return automatically afterward** is an explicit RP alternate-history device. It is not a representation of Greece's actual 1974 step, and the documented Greek return to the integrated command came in 1980, not automatically at the end of the Cyprus war. The North Atlantic Treaty says members consult under Article 4 when one considers an ally's integrity, independence or security threatened. Article 5 describes each ally taking the action it deems necessary after a covered armed attack; the text does not supply a ready-made game event that automatically makes every ally fight in a Greece-Turkey conflict. Article 13 sets out a one-year notice process for leaving the Treaty. These clauses can inform diplomatic framing, but the game should not present the requested immediate exit and automatic return as historical procedure or as a settled legal conclusion. [North Atlantic Treaty, Articles 4, 5 and 13](https://www.nato.int/en/about-us/official-texts-and-resources/official-texts/1949/04/04/the-north-atlantic-treaty).

## Bounded design recommendations

- Turkey may receive a **temporary navy or planning bonus** as a balance incentive before a choice to intervene. Describe it as readiness or operational preparation, not as a quantified historical capability. The 1964 and 1967 episodes establish credible threat and crisis planning; they do not prove that a specific modifier or guaranteed advantage existed.
- If Turkey declines to intervene after a Cyprus coup and security crisis, an **Angry Army** penalty lasting a few months and a subsequent coup-risk event/decision chain can be an interesting alternative-history cost. Label that chain as a fictional balance choice. The March 1971 memorandum is only a broad Turkish military-politics precedent. No cited source establishes that nonintervention in Cyprus in July 1974 would have caused a coup or an army ultimatum.
- Keep the Turkish intervention, Greek assistance to Cyprus, and Greek-Turkish war as separate choices with their own country-event responses. Greece's option to send volunteers to assist Cyprus and its option to declare war on Turkey are alternate histories; the latter did not happen in 1974. The real Greek government ordered general mobilization, while the Greek junta collapsed on 23 July. Avoid turning either branch into a scripted historical inevitability. [Sisco's 20 July account](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v30/d104); [UNFICYP 1974 chronology](https://unficyp.unmissions.org/en/events-summer-1974).
- If the design includes full temporary NATO exits for **both** countries and automatic return after hostilities, call this out in the RP explanation as alternate history. A monthly relationship reconciliation can resolve changed relations after player choices without implying an automatic NATO treaty process. Keep country consent explicit and keep the Treaty of Guarantee's disputed application separate from a game-triggered war.
